A B S T R A C T  
A key preprocessing step in tumor recognition based on microarray expression profile data and machine learning 
is to identify tumor marker genes. Gene selection aims to select the most relevant gene subset from the original 
ultra-high dimensional microarray expression profile data to improve tumor identification performance. Inspired 
by evolutionary multitasking (EMT) and multi-objective optimization, this paper puts forward a novel multi­
tasking multi-objective differential evolution gene selection algorithm (MMODE) which uses new elite and 
guidance strategies to select the best gene subsets. MMODE initializes two different populations according to 
different filtering criteria to increase the diversity of the search. These two populations guide their respective 
populations to search in the optimal direction through knowledge transfer in the evolutionary process. In 
addition, MMODE employs new elite and guidance strategies that enables individuals to narrow the search range 
and jump out of local optima. The proposed algorithm is validated on 13 publicly available microarray 
expression datasets in comparison with state-of-the-art gene selection algorithms. The experimental results show 
that MMODE can find smaller gene subsets and achieve higher classification accuracy compared with other 
algorithms.   
1. Introduction 
The emergence and rapid development of large-scale gene expression 
profiling provide a new technology platform for tumor research. The 
features in microarray gene expression profile data are called genes, and 
the process of selecting a subset of features from these data that are most 
relevant for disease diagnosis is called gene selection. Tumor gene se­
lection based on microarray expression profile data is a hot and difficult 
topic in the field of machine learning and data mining. Microarray gene 
expression profile data are characterized by high dimensionality (tens of 
thousands) and a small number of samples (dozens of), but few genes are 
related to disease diagnosis. How to identify the most relevant gene 
subsets from ultra-high dimensional microarray expression profile data 
to improve the accuracy of tumor recognition, which brings great 
challenges to the task of gene selection (Deng, Li, Deng, & Wang, 2022; 
Ke et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). 
Classification of high-dimensional data usually requires dimension­
ality reduction to improve the generalization of classification models 
and reduce the consumption of computational resources. As a data 
preprocessing technology, feature selection (FS) (Ray, Reddy, & Bane­
rjee, 2021) reduces the number of features by removing irrelevant and 
redundant features, so as to achieve the purpose of improving the clas­
sification performance of learning model Chen, Yuan, Lv, & Xiang, 2021; 
Wang et al., 2017; Wang, Zhu, Li, Chen, & Zhang, 2021). Compared with 
feature extraction, FS can retain the physical meanings of the original 
feature space and improve the readability and interpretability of the 
learning model (Nie, Wang, Tian, Wang, & Li, 2022). Due to its effec­
tiveness, FS has been widely used in data mining, such as cancer 
detection (Zibakhsh & Abadeh, 2013) and shop floor scheduling prob­
lems (Zhang, Mei, Nguyen, & Zhang, 2021). 
To date, many FS methods have been developed, which can be 
roughly divided into the filter, wrapper, and hybrid methods (Hancer, 
Xue,& Zhang, 2022; Li, Shang, Feng, & Fan, 2014; Tiwari & Chaturvedi, 
2022). The filter-based approaches searched for a subset of features 
based on characteristics of the features (e.g. similarity, correlation, etc.) 
(Ba, Wang, Yang, Yu, & Yu, 2023). The wrapper-based methods utilize a

based methods are computationally expensive. In contrast, filter-based 
methods are less costly but often less accurate than wrapper-based 
methods. On combine the advantages of filer-based and wrapper- 
based methods, filter-wrapper-based hybrid methods were proposed 
(Ghosh, Guha, Sarkar, & Abraham, 2020). The filter-wrapper-based 
methods achieve high classification accuracy with low time consump­

FS for high-dimensional data (Huang, Yang, Zhou, & Huang, 2019). 
Typically, a filter-wrapper-based approach includes two stages. The first 
stage uses a filter method to evaluate the importance of features and 
remove the most irrelevant and redundant features. Therefore, the re­
sults of the first stage are influenced by a filter being adopted. The 
second stage uses a wrapper-based approach to select the optimal 
feature subset from the remaining features, which belongs to a combi­
natorial optimization problem (Got, Moussaoui, & Zouache, 2021), and 
many swarm intelligence methods have been developed to solve this 
problem in recent years. 
Differential evolution (DE), which was developed by Storn and Price 
(1997), is one of the most effective evolutionary algorithms (Wang, Cai, 
& Zhang, 2011). So far, many DE-based FS methods have been proposed. 
For example, a hybrid FS method based on sine cosine algorithm (SCA) 
and DE was proposed by Abd Elaziz et al. (2017). This method uses 
differential evolution operator to improve the local search strategy and 
overcomes the shortcoming that SCA is easy to fall into the local opti­
mum. The history-guided differential evolution (HGDE) algorithm was 
recently proposed by Guan et al. (2021) for feature selection of high- 
dimensional data. It can remove duplicate individuals to maintain 
population diversity and guide the search direction of individuals ac­
cording to search history, thus reducing the probability of falling into 
local optimum. In fact, many scholars have focused on solving the 
problem that DE-based FS algorithms are prone to fall into local optima 
(i.e., individuals are stagnant) (Liu et al., 2019; Wang, Xue, Liang, & 
Zhang, 2021; Wang et al., 2015). However, for high-dimensional 
microarray gene expression profile data, it is a very difficult task to 
find the optimal solution due to its huge gene subset search space. In 
addition, FS is considered a multi-objective problem since its two ob­
jectives are to reduce the number of features and to improve the clas­
sification performance. How to find the optimal subset of genes from 
microarray expression data still needs further study. 
Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) can obtain groups 
of non-dominated solutions and are frequently used to solve multi- 
objective optimization problems (MOPs). At present, MOEAs can be 
divided into three categories, namely, dominance-based algorithms 
(Deb, Pratap, Agarwal, & Meyarivan, 2002; Palakonda & Mallipeddi, 
2017; Tian et al., 2019), decomposition-based algorithms (Han, Chen, 
Ling, & Han, 2021; Liao, Ishibuchi, & Meng Pang, 2020; Nguyen et al., 
2020), and indicator-based methods (Bader & Zitzler, 2011; Hong et al., 
2019; Zitzler & Künzli, 2004). However, these algorithms are also prone 
to fall into local optimum and premature convergence. In previous 
MOEAs, only one population is maintained during the population evo­
lution. The disadvantage of this is that the population potentially lacks 
diversity since the population tends to converge to a locally optimal 
position in a multi-objective optimization problem (Zhao, Liu, Zhang, & 
Liu, 2019). Compared with single-population evolution, multiple- 
populations increase the diversity of populations, make individuals 
more evenly distributed, and effectively overcome the local optimum 
problem. Note that MOEAs cannot solve multiple MOPs simultaneously. 
In other words, MOEAs can only solve one multi-objective problem at a 
time. However, multiple MOPs are interrelated with each other, mean­
ing that the beneficial knowledge gained in optimizing one MOPs can be 
used to solve other related problems. 
To effectively cope with the above problem, Gupta et al. (2016) 
proposed a new paradigm called evolutionary multitasking (EMT) to 
handle multiple optimization tasks simultaneously. Due to the potential 
connection between task populations, the EMT algorithm often makes it 
easier to achieve an optimum solution than the corresponding single- 
task population algorithm. The EMT paradigm has attracted much 
attention from MOPs researchers. For example, a multi-objective 
multifactorial evolutionary algorithm MO-MFEA (Gupta, Ong, Feng, & 
Tan, 2017) was proposed based on nondominated sorting genetic al­
gorithm II (NSGA-II) (Deb, Pratap, Agarwal, & Meyarivan, 2002). On 
this basis, Gupta proposed a new EMT algorithm, MO-MFEA-II (Bali, 
Gupta, Ong, & Tan, 2021), which improved the lack of information 
exchanged between tasks. In addition, Liang et al. (2022) proposed a 
new subspace alignment and adaptive DE algorithm for EMT, namely 
MOMFEA-SADE. This algorithm uses the mapping of subspaces to 
transform the search space of populations and reduce the probability of 
negative knowledge transfer between tasks. Recently, the EMT algo­
rithm has also been applied to feature selection. For example, Chen et al. 
(2021) proposed a PSO-based multi-task algorithm that can find a small 
feature subset in a reasonable time and achieve high classification ac­
curacy. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are very few FS 
applications of the EMT algorithm for high-dimensional data, which 
means that there is still research work to be done in this area. 
Inspired by the EMT paradigm for solving complex problems, this 
paper designs a novel Multitasking Multi-objective Differential Evolu­
tion gene selection algorithm (MMODE) which uses new elite and 
guidance strategies to select the best gene subsets. MMODE searches the 
optimal gene subset by knowledge transfer between two related FS task 
populations. Different from the traditional evolutionary multi-task 
involving different problems, two FS tasks in this study are composed 
of different feature weights in various filter criteria, and the different 
weights of features determine the different search directions of each 
task. Two FS tasks have the same search space but not the same search 
direction, which ensures the internal relationship of the tasks and fa­
cilitates the knowledge transfer between tasks. In this multitasking 
system, knowledge is transferred between two tasks so that the popu­
lation of one task can well guide the search for another task. In addition, 
to address the stagnation of individuals, a new guidance strategy is 
developed to cope with this situation. Furthermore, a new elitist strategy 
is adopted to reduce the search range, which effectively improves the 
performance of the algorithm for feature selection of high-dimensional 
data. For gene selection of microarray gene expression profile data, 
MMODE is promising to achieve high classification accuracy with a few 
genes. Specifically, the main contributions of this paper are outstanding 
as follows. 
1) The MMODE proposed in this paper is a new feature selection al­
gorithm based on multi-task DE algorithm. 
2) MMODE generates differentiated task populations, transfers knowl­
edge in the process of population evolution, adaptively changes 
mutation operators, and effectively enhances the diversity of 
populations.  
3) Different from the traditional multi-objective paradigm, MMODE 
uses a new elite strategy and a new guidance strategy to reduce the 
search space and avoid falling into local optima. In the elite strategy, 
a non-dominated ranking is used to generate elite populations for 
multi-task evolution. The proposed guidance strategy can help 
stagnant individuals jump out of local optima.  
4) The effectiveness of MMODE was validated by comparing it with a 
variety of state-of-the-art algorithms on 13 gene microarray datasets. 
Experimental results show that MMODE outperforms the comparison 
algorithms in terms of classification accuracy and number of selected 
features.

2. Related work 
In this section, some related knowledges are briefly reviewed. Sec­
tion 2.1 introduces the filter method used by MMODE. Section 2.2 in­

2.1. Filter criteria 
FS of high-dimensional data is extremely challenging due to a large 
number of redundant and irrelevant features, so promising subsets of 
features need to be identified quickly (i.e., a subset needs to include 
important features) to improve efficiency. Filter methods are popular 
due to their ability to extract a promising subset of features simply and 
quickly. Among them, ReliefF (Spolaor, Cherman, Monard, & Lee, 2013) 
and mutual information (Hancer, Xue, & Zhang, 2018) are widely used 
in the field of data mining because they can effectively determine the 
relationship between features and classes. In this study, ReliefF and 
mutual information are used to assign different weights to candidate 
features of the two task populations. 
2.1.1. ReliefF 
The Relief (Kononenko, 1994) algorithm is a nearest-neighbor-based 
feature evaluation algorithm, which is widely used to solve binary 
classification problems. ReliefF is an extension of Relief to handle multi- 
classification problems. According to ReliefF, the weights of features are 
evaluated based on their ability to distinguish the sampled instances 
from their neighbors in the same class and neighboring instances in 
different classes. The pseudo-code for calculating feature weights using

2.4. Evolutionary multitasking for multi-objective optimization 
Most of the existing evolutionary algorithms are used to deal with a 
single task (including single-objective optimization problems and multi- 
objective optimization problems), with less research into solving mul­
tiple tasks simultaneously. Gupta et al. (2016) applied to multitask 
learning to evolutionary computing and proposed a paradigm evolu­
tionary multitasking (EMT) for evolutionary computing. In evolutionary 
multitask learning, multiple tasks can transfer knowledge to each other 
when dealing with similar problems. The knowledge of one task is 
transferred to the target task, and the performance of the target task is 
improved. This kind of interactive learning between tasks is called 
transfer learning. Multi-task learning improves the performance of all 
tasks based on the inherent relationships between tasks, each task 
benefits from the knowledge gained by the other tasks. 
EMT algorithms tend to obtain better solutions for the optimization 
tasks than the counterpart single-task-based algorithms and achieved 
success in various optimization problems including MOPs. Multi- 
objective multifactorial evolutionary algorithm (MO-MFEA) (Gupta, 
Ong, Feng, & Tan, 2017) as an approach to solving MOPs, which solves 
MOPs simultaneously using the knowledge gained from each task. In the 
MO-MFEA framework, each task Tk will be considered as a factor 
affecting the individual in the search process, while the individuals in 
the task population Pk are encoded by a unique method that facilitates 
interactive problem solving. Thus, an individual Xi of the population Pk 
can be denoted as Pk(Xi) which can be represented as a solution in a 
special decoding way. 
To allow all tasks to transfer knowledge with each other, a unified 
encoding is used to control the search scope of all tasks in the same 
search space. The implicit connection can be generated by all tasks being 
in the uniform search space. Transferring knowledge between tasks is 
determined by the parameter of random mating probability (RMP), 
which can be set to a value close to 1 when the correlation between two 
tasks is high, and a low value (usually close to 0) when the correlation 
between two tasks is low. This means that MO-MFEA is strongly 
dependent on the experimenter’s prior knowledge about the tasks be­
tween them. To overcome this deficiency, Gupta et al. proposed MO- 
MFEA-II which generates the RMP matrix (Eq.(12)) based on the adap­
tive learning of the probability distribution of the respective task 
population.

3. Proposed method 
For FS of high-dimensional data, evolutionary algorithms often face 
the problem of individual stagnation due to the overly broad search 
scope. As the number of stagnant individuals increases, such algorithms 
will fall into local optimum and premature convergence. 
In this section, a novel multitasking multi-objective differential 
evolution gene selection algorithm (MMODE) is presented in detail. The 
principles of MMODE include differentiated populations, elite strategy, 
multitasking population evolution, and guidance strategy, which are 
described in section 3.1 to 3.4, respectively. In section 3.5, the overall 
framework of MMODE is described. 
3.1. Differentiated populations 
When multitasking differential evolution algorithm is used to solve 
the optimal solution, the correlation between tasks will have an 
important influence on the result. MMODE introduces two differentiated 
populations to enhance the ability to search the optimal subsets in the 
high-dimensional feature space. Specifically, two populations (corre­
sponding two tasks) are designed to search the same feature space in 
different directions to increase the diversity of the populations, thus 
improving the performance of the algorithm.

range is between [0, 1], there will be some features whose weight be­
comes 1 after normalized, causing all individual to select the feature at 
initialization; or the weight becomes 0, then the feature weight is too 
low thus causing the feature to be ignored. In this study, T1 and T2 were 
set to 0.2 and 0.5 to scale the value between [0.2, 0.7], which not only 
ensures that the probability of each feature being selected is positively 
correlated with its weight, but also avoids the situation that some fea­
tures are completely selected or excluded. One might ask why the weight 
values are not mapped to other interval ranges, such as [0.1,0.8]. In fact, 
this range only ensures that features with lower weights have a lower 
probability of being selected (but avoid being completely excluded) and 
features with higher weights have a higher probability of being selected 
(but avoid necessarily being selected). Our experiments show that there 
is no significant difference in the experimental results under the con­
ditions of T1 ∈[0.1, 0.3] and T1 + T2 ∈[0.5, 0.8]. The reason should be 
attributed to the evolutionary mechanism of differential evolution al­
gorithm adopted. 
Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 respectively show the calculation principles of the 
corresponding feature weights of the two populations. The probability of 
each feature being selected is different in different population in­
dividuals because the corresponding feature weight W of each task is 
different. Moreover, even within the same population, individual 
chooses features differently due to randomness. If the i th dimension of 
an individual is initialized to a randomly generated value between 0 and 
Wi, that is [0, Wi], indicating that the i th feature is selected; otherwise, 
the i th feature is not selected. The initialization strategies for population 
I and population II are similar, except that the feature weights are 
different, resulting in different directions for initializing the 
populations.

3.2. Elite strategy 
In recent years, some scholars have studied multi-objective differ­
ential evolutionary (MODE) algorithms based on NSGA-II framework to 
solve FS problems. However, as the non-domination proceeds, the 
number of identical individuals increases, which affects the diversity of 
the population. The selection operator of MODE is greedy selection (as 
shown in Eq.(8)), which selects a better solution between the trial vector 
and the target vector to survive in the offspring population. If the target 
vector falls into a local optimum, the generated trial vector is more likely 
to be worse than the target vector (according to the fitness value). Then 
the target vector survives into the offspring population, resulting in the 
parent population and offspring population having the same solution 
vector. Two identical solution vectors cannot dominate each other, so 
the two identical solutions survive to the next generation of populations. 
This reduces population diversity and leads to premature convergence. 
This means that the non-dominated processes in NSGA-II are not suitable 
for DE, because DE needs to explore new spaces through vector move­
ments, and too many identical vectors will only limit the performance of 
DE.

3.4. Guidance Strategy 
For evolutionary algorithms, individuals tend to enter a stagnant 
state in the process of evolution, which affects the exploration ability of 
the algorithm. In MMODE, a novel guidance strategy is proposed to 
alleviate this situation. The proposed guidance strategy uses state 
detection and feature shrinkage to make individuals jump out of stasis. 
To determine whether an individual is in a stagnant state, in 
MMODE, the individual has an age property with an initial value of 0. If 
an individual stops updating during a certain generation of evolution, its 
age attribute is increased by 1. If the age of an individual reache 
threshold maxAge, then the individual is in the stagnation stage. 
Similar to the Elite Strategy (Algorithm 2), the guidance strategy 
also aims to remove redundant features of the individual to achieve 
better classification performance. For the stagnant individuals, if their 
attribute HR == Ture (meaning there are redundant features, as in Al­
gorithm 2), the Guidance Strategy (Algorithm 5) removes the features 
with lower weight from the selected features of the individuals, to make 
the individuals jump out of the stopped state. 
In Algorithm 5, X is an input individual with a stagnant state. In line 
1, the features selected by the individual X are stored in variable r in 
ascending order of feature weight. In lines 2–6, the classification error 
rate of individual X after deleting feature r(j) is evaluated; if the clas­
sification error rate is unchanged or is decreased, the algorithm returns 
the corresponding individual of X −r(j) (i.e., individual X updated suc­
cessfully and termination guidance strategies); otherwise, the next 
feature in r is evaluated. Lines 7–8 mean that individual X cannot be 
updated by guidance strategy, and its property HR is assigned False 
means that the individual no longer participates in Elite Strategy (Al­
gorithm 2) and Guidance Strategy (Algorithm 5).

3.5. Overall framework of MMODE 
The proposed MMODE adopts two task populations to search the 
feature space. Fig. 6 shows the flowchart of the MMODE. As shown in 
Fig. 5, each task population has its initialization strategy to explore in 
different regions. After population initialization, the proposed Elite 
Strategy (Algorithm 2) is used to get the elite population E. The pop­
ulation G is generated through the parent population P and the elite 
population E for efficient non-dominated sorting (Zhang, Tian, Cheng, & 
Jin, 2015) (P denotes the parent task population, E denotes the elite 
population generated according to elite strategy, and G is then the result 
of efficient non-domination of P and E). 
After fast non-dominated sorting, the next generation population P is 
generated through the multitasking evolution of population G (knowl­
edge will transfer between two task populations). The transferred 
knowledge also affects the distribution of individuals within each task 
population and the direction of exploration. Once an individual is at a 
stagnant stage (individuals no longer evolve) in its evolution, then 
Guidance Strategy (Algorithm 5) will help the individual out of this 
state. When the termination condition is satisfied, the set of non- 
dominated solutions in both task populations is output. To reduce the 
influence of redundant features, individuals in the non-dominated so­
lution set will be re-executed to guidance strategy. 
The pseudo-code of MMODE is shown in Algorithm 6. In lines 1––4, 
the two task populations are initialized according to the information 
entropy weight WIs and reliefF weight Wr of the features, respectively. 
The evolutionary search process of the two task populations is 
completed in lines 5–13, which roughly includes three parts. Part I (lines

stagnant individuals. After the population evolution is completed, in line

4.1. Experimental settings 
4.1.1. Datasets 
All experiments run on eleven publicly available microarray 
expression profile datasets. The characteristics of these datasets are 
listed in Table 1, where “Features”, “Classes” and “Instances” denote the

For a fair comparison, all data are normalized, and all compared 
algorithms use a uniform filter approach (the top 3 % of genes are 
selected by using ReliefF) for a fair comparison. 
Considering that KNN (Dudani, 1976) is a classically and commonly 
used classifier and is often used in gene selection tasks (Wang, Xue, 
Liang, & Zhang, 2022; Wang, Gao, Zhang, & Guo, 2022; Wang et al., 
2022), KNN (K = 10) is used as the evaluation classifier in the wrapper 
evaluation stage of all the comparison algorithms for a fair comparison. 
To avoid large biases, 10-fold cross-validation is adopted to evaluate the 
accuracy of the gene set obtained by each algorithm. In addition, each 
comparison method is not a deterministic heuristic algorithm, each of 
them is run independently 30 times on each dataset, and the average 
results are then compared to further reduce the impact of the variance. 
4.1.2. Compared algorithms 
We compare the proposed MMODE algorithm with feature selection 
algorithms based on evolutionary algorithms. These algorithms include 
three single-objective algorithms of JADE-NSO (Zeng, Zhang, Chen, & 
Hong, 2021), HPSO-LS (Moradi & Gholampour, 2016), HLBDA (Too & 
Mirjalili, 2021), and six multi-objective algorithms of NSGA-II, GR- 
MOEA (Liu, Chang, & Cheng, 2021), MOBGA-AOS (Xue, Zhu, Liang, & 
Słowik, 2021), MOFS-BDE (Zhang et al., 2020), CMODE (Pan, Liu, & 
Chu, 2022), and NMDE (Wang, Xue, Liang, & Zhang, 2022). The brief 
information on all compared algorithms is as follows. 
JADE-NSO: JADE-NSO as an improved algorithm of DE, utilizes the 
idea of deep search to FS. When an individual is at stagnation, the in­
dividual is reset to the historically optimal solution to continue the 
search. 
HPSO-LS: The first stage of HPSO-LS filters out a subset of irrelevant 
features. Then, the second stage of HPSO-LS utilizes particle swarm 
optimization (PSO) to search the optimal feature subset from the filtered 
dataset, in which individuals perform a local search based on feature 
similarity. 
HLBDA: HLBDA is a recently proposed algorithm especially for 
dealing with gene selection problems based on a binary dragonfly al­
gorithm (BDA). It uses hyper learning strategies to enable BDA to escape 
local optima and enhance search behavior. 
NSGA-II: NSGA-II is one of the most classical multi-objective algo­
rithms and is also used in FS since its efficient performance. Each iter­
ation produces the next population based on the non-dominated sorting 
of the parent and offspring populations. 
GR-MOEA: GR-MOEA is a multi-objective FS algorithm based on the 
NSGA-II paradigm, which inherits the advantages of NSGA-II. The filter 
and wrapper populations in the GR-MOEA influence each other during 
evolution according to guidance and repair strategies. 
MOBGA-AOS: MOBGA-AOS is one of the variants of NSGA-II for FS, 
which uses penalty and reward matrices to evolve individuals from 
adaptive crossover operators during iteration. 
MOFS-BDE: MOFS-BDE is a novel binary multi-objective differential 
evolutionary algorithm for FS. It improves the one-bit purifying search 
operator (OPS) and the probability difference-based binary mutation 
operator, making DE a strong exploration and exploitation capability. 
CMODE: CMODE as a pareto-dominance-based multi-objective DE 
for FS, focuses on ensuring diversity of solutions. CMODE selects elite 
individuals based on the competition mechanism and elite individuals to 
guide the evolution of other individuals through a novel mutation 
operator. 
NMDE: NMDE is a multi-objective FS algorithm based on the niche 
theories. In the framework of evolutionary multi-objective optimization, 
NMDE uses a mutation operator that combines local and global infor­
mation of individual nearest neighbor, which effectively speeds up the 
convergence of the algorithm. 
The latter 6 algorithms are all multi-objective algorithms. All of them 
use efficient non-dominated sorting (Zhang, Tian, Cheng, & Jin, 2015) 
for multi-objective comparison for the sake of fairness. 
4.1.3. Parameter settings 
All comparison algorithms use the recommended parameter values 
suggested in their original papers. The parameter values of MMODE and 
all compared algorithms are listed in Table 2. 
There are two task populations in MMODE and GR-MOEA, the 
number of individuals in each task population is 70. To fair comparison, 
in all other eight compared evolutionary algorithms, the population size 
m is set to 140, which makes all 10 algorithms use the same number of 
individuals for searching feature subsets. In addition, the maximum

4.2.2. Analysis of strategy effectiveness 
In this section, we verify the effectiveness of the Elite Strategy and 
Guidance Strategy used in MMODE in detail. Specifically, we compare 
MMODE with MMODE-A, MMODE-B, and MMODE-C, which are all 
variants of the original MMODE. 
MMODE-A: The variant retains the overall framework of MMODE 
but discards the Elite Strategy and the Guidance Strategy. 
MMODE-B: The variant retains the overall framework of MMODE 
but does not adopt the Elite Strategy. 
MMODE-C: The variant retains the overall framework of MMODE 
but does not adopt the Guidance Strategy. 
The three MMODE variants above generate offspring populations 
through multitasking population evolution. Table 6 shows the compar­
ison of experimental results between MMODE and its three variants on 
13 datasets concerning the Mean Time (s), Mean Size (the number of 
selected features), Best Accuracy, and Mean Accuracy. 
As can be seen from Table 6, the MMODE-A variant has the worst 
performance on all datasets in terms of Best Accuracy and Mean Accu­
racy, except for the Leukemia, Lung cancer, and Ovarian datasets, where 
the MMODE-A variant performs as well as the other algorithms. In 
addition, the MMODE-A variant obtains the worst Mean Size (the 
average number of selected features per dataset is the highest) of the 
gene subset. Note that the MMODE-A variant does not use the Elite 
Strategy and the Guidance Strategy, while the effectiveness of the 
proposed Elite Strategy and the Guidance Strategy is verified on all 
datasets. 
Note that the MMODE-C variant uses the Elite Strategy but does not 
use the Guidance Strategy, and the MMODE-B variant uses the Guid­
ance Elite Strategy but does not use the Elite Strategy. The comparison 
of the experimental results between the MMODE-B variant and the 
MMODE-C variant allows the analysis of the roles and differences be­
tween the Guidance Strategy and the Elite Strategy. Table 6 shows 
that compared with MMODE-B, the MMODE-C variant has the better 
performance on all datasets in terms of Best Accuracy and Mean Accu­
racy, except for the Leukemia, Lung cancer, and Ovarian datasets, where 
the MMODE-B variant performs as well as the MMODE-C. In addition, 
the MMODE-C variant obtains the better Mean Size (the average 
numbers of selected features for almost datasets are shorter) of the gene 
subset than MMODE-B. This result indicates that Elite Strategy is more 
effective than the Guidance Strategy. 
Compared with the Guidance Strategy (MMODE-B), although Elite 
Strategy (MMODE-C) achieves better results both in the classification 
accuracy and the number of selected features, the role of the Elite 
Strategy cannot replace the Guidance Strategy. As described in Section 
3.4, the Guidance Strategy plays a key role in helping the stagnant 
individuals jump out of the local optima, removing redundant features, 
and improving the performance of the algorithm. Compared with three 
variants of MMODE-A, MMODE-B, and MMODE-C, the proposed 
MMODE combines Elite Strategy and Guidance Strategy, so it has the 
advantages of both the Elite Strategy and the Guidance Strategy. 
According to Table 6, MMODE obtains the best results both in the 
classification accuracy and the number of selected features compared 
with the three variants. 
To further show the difference between MMODE and its three vari­
ants in the feature selection process, Fig. 8 shows the Pareto front sub­
sets obtained by them on 8 datasets. As shown in Fig. 8, the subset of 
Pareto fronts obtained by MMODE locates in the bottom left corner of 
each sub-figure, which means that MMODE selects the least number of 
features and has the lowest classification error rate. In addition, Fig. 8 
intuitively shows that MMODE is better than MMODE-C and MMODE-B, 
and MMODE-C and MMODE-B are better than MMODE-A. In short, 
MMODE obtains the smallest gene subset and the best classification 
performance by using the Elite Strategy and the Guidance Strategy, 
which fully proves the effectiveness of the Elite Strategy and the 
Guidance Strategy. 
4.2.3. Sensitivity analysis of parameters in MMODE 
There are three important parameters in the proposed algorithm 
MMODE, which are ep (the elite rate) and L(the probability of weakly 
correlated features being selected) for controlling the Elite Strategy, 
and maxAge (the upper limit of the age at which individuals stagnate) for 
controlling the Guidance Strategy. We empirically investigate the ef­
fect of parameters ep, L and maxAge on the MMODE. 
To obtain the appropriate parameters, we implemented Taguchi’s 
experimental design (Altland, 2006). Taguchi’s experimental design is a 
technique that uses orthogonal arrays to create testing cases that assist 
in determining the parameters with minimal effort (Gao et al., 2019). 
The value of the parameter ep is chosen from the array [0.02, 0.08, 0.1,
0.2, 0.3], the value of the parameter L is chosen from the array 
[0.003, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1] and the value of the parameter maxAge is 
chosen from the array [3, 6, 9, 12, 15]. Therefore, an orthogonal array 
L25(53) was used for experiments to represent the combination of 
parameter values. The 25 parameter sets are listed in Table 7. To 
demonstrate the average classification accuracy and the average number 
of selected features of MMODE with different parameter sets, they are 
ranked according to Score (Eq.15), and the top 5 parameter sets are

5. Conclusion 
In the classification of microarray gene expression profile data, 
existing multi-objective evolutionary algorithms are prone to local 
optimality in feature (gene) selection. In this study, a new gene selection 
algorithm MMODE is proposed, aiming to select a few genes in tumor 
classification and achieve higher accuracy. The main contributions of 
the proposed MMODE include the following aspects. (1) An efficient 
initialization strategy is proposed to effectively differentiate between 
task populations. The strategy enables specifying the search direction of 
the different task populations. (2) The final feature subset with higher 
quality is obtained based on the knowledge transfer of the two task 
populations. (3) MMODE uses a new elite strategy and a new guidance 
strategy to reduce the search space and avoid falling into local optima. 
The effectiveness of MMODE is validated by comparing it with state- 
of-the-art algorithms on a 13-gene microarray dataset. The experimental 
results demonstrate that MMODE is a promising approach for solving 
gene selection problems. Because MMODE shows better or at least 
competitive performance in terms of classification accuracy and number 
of selected genes compared with other algorithms. Moreover, based on 
their reported results, MMODE also shows superiority over other 
evolutionary algorithms from the literature. All these suggest that the 
proposed elite and guiding strategies can optimize the search space of 
the population, improve the quality of individuals in the population, and 
help the algorithm to escape from local optima. 
Some further studies related to MMODE will be carried out. First, we 
will extend MMODE to solve other real-world problems in domains with 
multiple optimal solution requirements. Second, we continue to opti­
mize and improve the time consumption and data space occupation of 
MMODE. Finally, this study confirms that the method of co-evolution of
