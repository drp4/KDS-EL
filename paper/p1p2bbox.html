Multi-Task Particle Swarm Optimization with Knowledge-Guided Elite Refinement and Two-Phase Weight Scheduling for High-Dimensional Feature Selection Anonymous Author a a Anonymous Institution (masked for double-blind review), Abstract Feature selection in high-dimensional data remains a central challenge for evolutionary computation. Particle swarm optimization suffers from three structural deficiencies: random initialization provides insufficient guidance in sparse high-dimensional spaces, uniform mutation discards cross-iteration feature-importance signals, and static objective preferences cannot adapt to different evolutionary phases. To address these limitations, we propose KDS-EL (Knowledge-Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning), a closed-loop knowledge-guided evolutionary framework. First, to overcome cold-start inefficiency, we propose mutual-information-guided initialization with adaptive re-initialization, injecting prior knowledge and recovering population diversity when stagnation occurs. Second, to exploit discarded cross-iteration feature-importance signals, we propose a knowledge-guided elite refinement strategy that accumulates feature-importance knowledge and steers elite solutions toward historically effective features. Third, to align objective weights with search phases, we propose two-phase weight scheduling that emphasizes early dimensionality reduction and late-stage accuracy refinement. On 20 high-dimensional benchmarks, KDS-EL ranks first among all competitors, achieving a mean accuracy of 0.9574 with only 87.8 selected features, outperforming the strongest single- and multi-objective competitors by +3.97 and +4.42 percentage points; Wilcoxon and Holm–Bonferroni tests confirm statistically significant improvements over all 13 competitors. Introduction High-dimensional data have emerged as a fundamental challenge in clas- sification problems across diverse domains, including genomics, text mining, and computer vision. These datasets typically contain from hundreds to mil- lions of features, while only a limited number of labeled samples are available, resulting in the well-known p ≫ n scenario. Under such conditions, conven- tional learning algorithms often suffer from severe overfitting, increased com- putational complexity, and reduced interpretability due to the curse of dimen- sionality [1, 2]. Feature selection (FS) has therefore become an essential pre- processing technique that aims to identify a compact and informative subset of features while preserving classification performance. However, selecting an op- timal feature subset remains highly challenging because the search space grows exponentially with the number of features D, and FS is an NP-hard combinato- rial optimization problem [3]. Moreover, individual feature relevance measures are insufficient to characterize complex feature interactions and dependencies, which are often critical for achieving robust classification performance. FS methods are generally categorized into filter, wrapper, and embedded approaches [1, 4]. Filter methods rank features according to statistical criteria and offer high computational efficiency, but they usually evaluate features inde- pendently of the classifier and thus ignore classifier-specific relevance. Embed- ded methods incorporate feature selection into the learning process, providing a compromise between selection efficiency and predictive performance; however, their effectiveness is often dependent on the characteristics of specific learning models. Wrapper methods directly optimize feature subsets according to clas- sifier performance and can achieve competitive classification results, but the repeated model evaluations make them computationally expensive, especially in high-dimensional search spaces. To address the combinatorial complexity of wrapper-based FS, evolutionary computation has been extensively investigated as a promising optimization paradigm [5–7]. By maintaining a population of candidate solutions, evolutionary algorithms can perform global exploration of feature subsets without requiring gradient information. Among them, particle swarm optimization (PSO), genetic algorithms, and differential evolution have demonstrated promising results on high-dimensional FS tasks. PSO has become one of the most widely adopted evolutionary algorithms for FS due to its simple structure, efficient information-sharing mechanism, and fast convergence. Nevertheless, conventional PSO-based FS methods still encounter several structural limitations when dealing with sparse and high-dimensional search spaces. First, random initialization distributes particles without prior guidance throughout the binary search space, whereas effective feature subsets usually occupy only a small region with limited active dimensions. Consequently, a considerable number of fitness evaluations may be wasted on redundant and suboptimal solutions before the swarm identifies Email address: corresponding.author@example.com (Anonymous Author) promising search regions. Second, conventional uniform bit-flip mutation treats all dimensions equally and neglects the feature-level improvement information accumulated during previous evaluations, preventing the search from exploiting historically informative features. Third, existing PSO-based FS methods typically employ fixed objective weights to balance classification error and feature ratio during the entire optimization process. However, the desired trade-off between feature reduction and accuracy refinement varies across search stages: aggressive sparsity enhancement is preferred in early exploration, whereas accurate decision boundary refinement becomes more important after a compact subset has been obtained. Recent evolutionary FS studies have attempted to improve search perfor- mance through enhanced population initialization, adaptive evolutionary op- erators, and refined fitness evaluation strategies [8–11]. Although these ap- proaches have achieved promising results, they usually focus on isolated com- ponents of the optimization process and lack a unified mechanism for improv- ing early-stage exploration, exploiting historical feature information, and adapt- ing optimization objectives throughout evolution. Recently, multi-task PSO has emerged as an effective paradigm for high-dimensional FS with fixed-length binary representations. For instance, MEL [12] adopts cooperative subpopula- tions and a fixed-length binary encoding strategy to efficiently handle feature spaces containing tens of thousands of dimensions. Despite its effectiveness, existing multi-task PSO-based FS frameworks still leave several challenges un- resolved: the initial population is generally generated without sufficient feature- level prior knowledge, useful feature-importance information discovered during evolution is not explicitly retained and exploited, and the balance between clas- sification performance and feature sparsity remains unchanged across different search stages. To address these limitations, this paper proposes KDS-EL (Knowledge- Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning), a closed-loop knowledge- guided evolutionary framework that enables knowledge acquisition, accumula- tion, and exploitation throughout the multi-task PSO search process. The pro- posed framework integrates three complementary mechanisms that address dif- ferent stages of high-dimensional FS optimization. First, mutual-information-guided initialization with adaptive re- initialization incorporates prior feature-relevance knowledge into population construction and reduces the search waste caused by unguided initialization in the early stage. Second, the Knowledge-guided Elite Refinement (KER) strategy accumulates cross-iteration feature-importance knowledge from the fitness comparisons already performed during search, applies exponential decay to reduce the influence of outdated evidence, and guides elite solutions toward historically informative features within a bounded refinement budget. Third, Two-Phase Weight Scheduling dynamically adjusts the dual-objective preference from feature reduction to classification refinement, matching optimization priorities with the evolving requirements of high-dimensional FS. These mechanisms operate at complementary levels of the evolutionary process, and their individual contributions can be systematically verified through component contribution analysis. Single-Objective Evolutionary Feature Selection Evolutionary algorithms have been extensively applied to feature selec- tion for their population-based global search and gradient-free nature. Early work adapted classical meta-heuristics—binary PSO (BPSO) [7], ant colony optimization [21], artificial bee colony [22], and genetic algorithms with sup- port vector machines [23]—to the binary subset selection problem, establish- ing the template of a population of candidate subsets evolving under selection, crossover, and mutation guided by a scalar fitness function. Because standard evolutionary operators degrade at high dimensionality, specialized single-objective methods have been developed: self-adaptive PSO adjusting swarm size and mutation rate [24], progressively refined PSO al- ternating between coarse- and fine-grained search [25], and problem-specific heuristics—FWPSO [8], which biases velocity updates with per-feature im- portance; SaWDE [9] with adaptive weighting; IGPSO [10], which estimates relevance via a multi-layer perceptron; and PSO-CSM [11] integrating mul- tiple evaluation criteria. Learning-based control and space decomposition are also explored: reinforcement-learning-aided PSO [26] and divide-and-conquer search [27]. These methods refine search operators but do not systematically exploit accumulated feature-importance knowledge harvested from the search itself. 1. We propose an MI-guided initialization scheme with adaptive re-initialization, which incorporates feature-relevance knowledge into population construction and restores search diversity when evolutionary stagnation occurs. 2. We propose the Knowledge-guided Elite Refinement (KER) strategy, which accumulates cross-iteration feature-importance evidence from the fitness comparisons already performed during search, reduces the influence of obsolete knowledge through exponential decay, and per- forms knowledge-guided elite refinement within a bounded refinement budget. 3. We propose Two-Phase Weight Scheduling (TPS), which replaces static objective weighting with a dynamic optimization strategy consisting of an early feature-reduction phase and a later accuracy-refinement phase, thereby aligning optimization preferences with the temporal character- istics of high-dimensional FS. 4. Extensive evaluations, including component contribution analysis and parameter sensitivity analysis, are performed to validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework. 2.3. Mutual-Information-Guided and Importance-Aware Evolutionary Meth- ods Extensive experiments demonstrate that KDS-EL achieves the best over- all performance, obtaining an average classification accuracy of 0.9574 with only 87.8 selected features. Compared with the strongest single-objective and multi-objective competitors, KDS-EL improves accuracy by 3.97 and 4.42 per- centage points, respectively, while achieving a 3.4× speedup over MEL (51.4 s vs. 175.0 s). Furthermore, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction confirm that the improvements are statistically significant over all 13 competitors. Mutual information (MI) quantifies the reduction in label uncertainty pro- vided by a feature [28] and is the most widely used relevance score in hybrid filter-wrapper feature selection. In filter form, mRMR [15] and FCBF [16] rank features before any classifier is trained, offering efficient coarse pruning at the price of ignoring feature interactions, as discussed in Section 2.1. Within evo- lutionary wrappers, MI and related importance scores have also been used to shape the search itself: Yu et al. [29] rank features by MI and initialize a multi- population differential evolution with the top-ranked subset; FWPSO biases velocity updates with per-feature importance scores [8]; and IGPSO learns a feature-importance estimate with a multilayer perceptron [10]. These methods demonstrate that prior relevance knowledge can improve the starting point or update direction of evolutionary search. Such filter-driven schemes, however, consume the injected knowledge one-shot: MI rankings are computed before optimization and are never revised by the search itself. The per-feature evidence accumulated across iterations—which additions or removals improved fitness—is discarded, and subsequent operators remain uniform. KDS-EL instead treats feature- importance knowledge as an in-loop state: MI scores bootstrap the initial population, while the KER strategy maintains a cumulative feature-importance matrix that is updated from the fitness comparisons already performed during search and re-deployed to refine elite solutions within a bounded refinement budget. 2. Related Work 2.1. Feature Selection Paradigms Feature selection is a fundamental pre-processing step in high-dimensional data analysis, aiming to identify a minimal subset of relevant features that preserves or improves classification performance while reducing computational cost and enhancing interpretability [1, 2]. Methods fall broadly into three paradigms: filter, wrapper, and embedded. Filter methods evaluate features independently of any learning algorithm, relying on intrinsic data properties such as variance, correlation, or eigenvec- tor centrality [13, 14]. Representative techniques include the minimum Redun- dancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) criterion [15] and the Fast Correlation- Based Filter (FCBF) [16]. These methods are computationally efficient and scale readily to high-dimensional settings, yet they disregard feature interac- tions and may retain redundant subsets under strong inter-feature correlation. Wrapper methods treat feature selection as a search problem, evaluating candidate subsets by the predictive performance of a specific classifier [4]. Hybrid genetic algorithms [17] and other meta-heuristic wrappers can capture complex feature interdependencies, but their computational cost grows rapidly with dimensionality, as each candidate subset requires a full classifier training- and-evaluation cycle. Embedded methods integrate feature selection into model training. Lasso and its bootstrapped variant Bolasso [18] enforce sparsity through L 1 regular- ization; gradient-boosting-based approaches such as FS-GBDT [19] leverage tree-split importance; and hybrid algorithms like ILRC [20] combine regular- ization with clustering. Embedded methods offer a favorable speed–accuracy trade-off, but their feature rankings are tied to specific model assumptions. At high dimensionality, each paradigm faces distinct structural bot- tlenecks: filters are fast but oblivious to feature interactions, wrappers are accurate but face a search space that grows exponentially with dimension, and embedded methods depend on model-specific regularization biases. These complementary weaknesses motivate evolutionary computation, which decouples the search mechanism from the evaluation metric and navigates the exploration–exploitation trade-off through population-based global search. 2.4. Multi-Objective Evolutionary Feature Selection Multi-objective feature selection (MOFS) treats classification accuracy and feature count as competing objectives, producing a Pareto front of non-dominated solutions. Xue et al. [30] pioneered multi-objective PSO for feature selection, showing that jointly optimizing accuracy and subset size yields more diverse and informative solutions than scalarized approaches. Hu et al. [31] extended this framework with fuzzy cost functions for imbalanced classification. The multi-objective evolutionary algorithm family provides several archi- tectures for this problem. NSGA-II-FS [32] uses non-dominated sorting and crowding distance to maintain Pareto-front diversity. MOEA/D-FS [33] de- composes the problem into scalar subproblems solved cooperatively. MOPSO- FS [34] extends single-objective PSO with an external archive and Pareto- dominance-based leader selection. Hancer et al. [35] combined evolutionary search with filter-based ranking under both single- and multi-objective settings. Despite their conceptual advantages, MOFS methods incur substantial overhead: maintaining an external archive, non-dominated sorting, and Pareto-front management all cost significant time (in our experiments, over 110 seconds on average). Moreover, under fixed-length binary encoding at high dimensionality, the Pareto front expands combinatorially and convergence slows, limiting scalability at gene-expression scale. Surrogate-assisted multi-objective search [36] can mitigate evaluation cost, yet surrogate accuracy and training overhead under extreme dimensionality remain open problems.
