A B S T R A C T
Feature selection in high-dimensional data remains a central challenge for evolutionary computation. Particle swarm
optimization suffers from three structural deficiencies: random initialization provides insufficient guidance in sparse
high-dimensional spaces, uniform feature perturbation fails to exploit cross-iteration feature-importance signals, and
static objective preferences cannot adapt to different evolutionary phases. To address these limitations, we propose
KDS-EL (Knowledge-Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning), a knowledge-guided evolutionary frame-
work with a closed-loop feature-importance learning mechanism. First, to overcome inefficient initialization, we pro-
pose mutual-information-guided initialization with adaptive re-initialization, injecting prior knowledge and recovering
population diversity when stagnation occurs. Second, to exploit discarded cross-iteration feature-importance signals,
we propose a knowledge-guided elite refinement strategy that accumulates feature-importance knowledge and steers
elite solutions toward historically effective features. Third, to align objective weights with search phases, we propose
two-phase weight scheduling that emphasizes early dimensionality reduction and late-stage accuracy refinement. On
20 high-dimensional benchmarks, KDS-EL achieves the best classification performance among all competitors, with a
mean accuracy of 0.9574 and only 87.8 selected features, outperforming the strongest single- and multi-objective com-
petitors by +3.97 and +4.42 percentage points; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction confirm
statistically significant improvements over all 13 competitors.
1. Introduction
High-dimensional data have emerged as a fundamental challenge in clas-
sification problems across diverse domains, including genomics, text mining,
and computer vision. These datasets typically contain hundreds or even tens
of thousands of features, while only a limited number of labeled samples are
available, resulting in the well-known p ≫n scenario. Under such conditions,
conventional learning algorithms often suffer from severe overfitting, increased
computational complexity, and reduced interpretability due to the curse of di-
mensionality [1, 2]. Feature selection (FS) has therefore become an essential
preprocessing technique that aims to identify a compact and informative sub-
set of features while preserving classification performance. However, select-
ing an optimal feature subset remains highly challenging because the search
space grows exponentially with the number of features D, and the underlying
subset-selection problem is NP-hard [3]. Moreover, individual feature relevance
measures are insufficient to characterize complex feature interactions and de-
pendencies, which are often critical for achieving robust classification perfor-
mance.
FS methods are generally categorized into filter, wrapper, and embedded
approaches [1, 4]. Filter methods rank features according to statistical criteria
and offer high computational efficiency, but they usually evaluate features in-
dependently of the classifier and may overlook feature interactions relevant to
classification. Embedded methods incorporate feature selection into the learn-
ing process, providing a compromise between selection efficiency and predic-
tive performance; however, their effectiveness is often dependent on the char-
acteristics of specific learning models. Wrapper methods directly optimize fea-
ture subsets according to classifier performance and can achieve competitive
classification results, but the repeated model evaluations make them computa-
tionally expensive, especially in high-dimensional search spaces. To address the
combinatorial complexity of wrapper-based FS, evolutionary computation has
been extensively investigated as a promising optimization paradigm [3, 5–7].
By maintaining a population of candidate solutions, evolutionary algorithms
can perform global exploration of feature subsets without requiring gradient
information. Among them, particle swarm optimization (PSO), genetic algo-
rithms, and differential evolution have demonstrated promising results on high-
dimensional FS tasks.
PSO has become one of the most widely adopted evolutionary algorithms
for FS due to its simple structure, efficient information-sharing mechanism,
and fast convergence. Nevertheless, conventional PSO-based FS methods
still encounter three structural limitations when dealing with sparse and
high-dimensional search spaces. First, random initialization distributes

becomes more important after a compact subset has been obtained.
Recent evolutionary FS studies have attempted to improve search perfor-
mance through enhanced population initialization, adaptive evolutionary opera-
tors, and refined fitness evaluation strategies [8–11]. Although these approaches
have achieved promising results, they usually focus on isolated components of
the optimization process and do not jointly address inefficient initialization, in-
sufficient exploitation of accumulated feature knowledge, and inflexible objec-
tive weighting. Recently, multi-task PSO has emerged as an effective paradigm
for high-dimensional FS with fixed-length binary representations. For instance,
MEL [12] adopts cooperative subpopulations and a fixed-length representation
to efficiently handle feature spaces containing tens of thousands of dimensions.
Despite its effectiveness, existing multi-task PSO-based FS frameworks still
leave several challenges unresolved: the initial population is generally gener-
ated without sufficient feature-level prior knowledge, useful feature-importance
information discovered during evolution is not explicitly retained and exploited,
and the balance between classification performance and feature sparsity re-
mains unchanged across different search stages.
To address these limitations, this paper proposes Knowledge-Guided Dual-
Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning (KDS-EL), a closed-loop framework that
synergistically integrates domain prior injection, cross-iteration knowledge ac-
cumulation, and dynamic objective scheduling into a cooperative multi-task
PSO backbone.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
1. MI-Guided Initialization with Adaptive Re-injection: A hybrid seed-
ing strategy that injects continuous feature-relevance priors into popu-
lation construction to eliminate early-stage search waste, coupled with
an adaptive re-injection mechanism that restores swarm diversity upon
evolutionary stagnation.
2. Knowledge-Guided Elite Refinement (KER): A targeted elite
perturbation operator that dynamically harvests cross-iteration feature-
importance feedback with exponential memory decay, directing elite
solutions toward historically effective features within a strictly bounded
evaluation overhead.

2.1. Evolutionary Swarm Intelligence for Feature Selection
Feature selection is a combinatorial NP-hard optimization problem that
aims to identify an optimal subset of d ≪D relevant features, maximizing pre-
dictive accuracy while eliminating redundant and uninformative dimensions [1,
2]. Traditional feature selection techniques are categorized into three classical
paradigms:
1. Filter methods: Filter techniques evaluate feature subsets based on in-
trinsic statistical properties, such as mutual information (mRMR [17]),
correlation (FCBF [18]), or graph-theoretic centrality [19]. Although
computationally efficient (O(D log D)), filter methods evaluate features
independently or pairwise, fundamentally ignoring complex multivari-
ate interactions and resulting in suboptimal accuracy when applied to
non-linear classification landscapes.
2. Wrapper methods: Wrapper approaches evaluate candidate subsets
using the direct predictive feedback of a machine learning classifier [4].
While wrappers capture intricate feature dependencies and deliver
superior classification performance, exhaustive evaluation is intractable
in high-dimensional spaces (2D combinatorial states), necessitating
stochastic global search heuristics.
3. Embedded
methods:
Embedded
methods
incorporate
feature
selection directly into model training via regularization, such as
LASSO/Bolasso [20] and tree-based ensemble importance (FS-
GBDT [21]). However, embedded rankings are tightly coupled with
specific classifier inductive biases and frequently struggle to generalize
across diverse classification architectures.
To harness the predictive fidelity of wrappers while mitigating combi-
natorial computational bottlenecks, Swarm Intelligence (SI) and Evolutionary
Computation (EC) have emerged as the dominant paradigms for feature selec-
tion [3, 22]. Classical meta-heuristics, including Binary Particle Swarm Opti-
mization (BPSO) [7], Genetic Algorithms (GA) [23], Ant Colony Optimization
(ACO) [24], and Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) [25], navigate high-dimensional
spaces via population-based stochastic exploration.
To combat the curse of dimensionality in small-sample regimes (p ≫n),
numerous specialized single-objective evolutionary algorithms have been
developed: self-adaptive swarm mechanisms [26], progressively refined
search trajectories [27], self-adaptive differential evolution with adaptive
weighting (SaWDE) [9], multi-layer perceptron-guided PSO (IGPSO) [10],
multi-criterion scoring PSO (PSO-CSM) [11], reinforcement-learning-assisted
swarm control [28], and divide-and-conquer subspace decomposition [29].
While these methods enhance local search operators, they universally treat fea-
ture perturbation as a memoryless stochastic trial, discarding the accumulated
evolutionary feedback across generations.
2.2. Multi-Objective versus Single-Objective Formulations at Scale
Feature selection naturally involves two conflicting objectives: minimiz-
ing classification error and minimizing the number of selected features. Multi-
Objective Feature Selection (MOFS) algorithms model this trade-off by search-
ing for a Pareto front of non-dominated solutions. Pioneered by Xue et al. [13],
diverse multi-objective evolutionary algorithms have been adapted to feature
selection, including NSGA-II-FS [30], MOEA/D-FS [31], MOPSO-FS [32],
and hybrid filter-wrapper multi-objective architectures [33, 34].
Despite their theoretical appeal, MOFS methods encounter severe opera-
tional limitations when scaled to ultra-high-dimensional biomedical data (D >
103):
1. Combinatorial archive and sorting overhead: Maintaining an exter-
nal non-dominated archive and executing Pareto ranking over dense
high-dimensional vectors incurs substantial computational overhead,
often exceeding 110–140 seconds in wall-clock time, as analyzed in
Section 5.7.
2. Pareto front dilution in ultra-high dimensions: In extreme p ≫n
regimes, non-dominated sorting tends to produce large, diffused Pareto
fronts populated by non-sparse candidate subsets, often retaining
2,000–5,000 active features, failing to deliver the ultra-sparse compact
biomarker signatures with fewer than 100 features required for clinical
interpretability.
To circumvent Pareto archive bloat and achieve extreme subset sparsity
within tight computational budgets, weighted single-objective scalarization re-
mains the preferred formulation in high-dimensional biomarker discovery [12,
16]. However, conventional weighted frameworks enforce fixed static weights

2.3. Evolutionary Multi-Task Optimization in Feature Selection
Evolutionary Multi-Task Optimization (EMTO) aims to solve mul-
tiple distinct optimization tasks simultaneously by facilitating inter-task
knowledge transfer through a unified chromosomal representation [35]. In
feature selection, EMTO frameworks partition the global population into
collaborative subpopulations to explore complementary search subspaces.
Prominent multi-task feature selection frameworks include minion-assisted
multi-tasking [36], evolutionary multi-task PSO for hyperspectral and gene
expression data [37, 38], multi-task bee colony band selection [39], and
large-scale multi-objective multi-tasking [40].
Most notably, Zhang et al. [12] introduced Multi-Task Evolutionary Learn-
ing (MEL), which establishes a dual-subpopulation PSO framework. In MEL,
Subpopulation 1 performs continuous swarm exploration guided by inter-task
attractors, while Subpopulation 2 conducts discrete feature sampling based on
a shared feature-importance matrix. While MEL successfully demonstrates the
power of cross-task knowledge transfer, its unguided multi-task formulation
suffers from three critical bottlenecks: (i) random uniform initialization that
causes unguided early-stage exploration in sparse spaces, (ii) unconstrained
feature importance accumulation without memory discounting, and (iii) rigid
static objective weights that delay early dimensionality reduction.
2.4. Knowledge-Guided Meta-Heuristics and Information-Theoretic Hy-
bridization
Integrating domain-specific prior knowledge into meta-heuristics has
attracted surging attention as a means to escape the blindness of black-box
search [14, 15, 41]. In high-dimensional feature selection, mutual information
(MI) serves as the primary information-theoretic metric to quantify non-linear
feature relevance [42].
Existing knowledge-guided evolutionary algorithms typically adopt one-
shot offline injection:
1. Static filter pre-filtering: Algorithms such as MI-MPODE [15] and
NNS-DFPG-CSA [14] compute MI rankings offline and initialize the
population or restrict the search space to the top-k ranked features.
2. Static velocity biasing: Frameworks such as FWPSO [8] incorporate
static feature-weight vectors to bias particle velocity equations through-
out evolution.
While beneficial, one-shot knowledge injection lacks online adaptability:
once injected, the static prior is never updated or refined by the empirical
trial-and-error feedback generated during subsequent wrapper evaluations.
Conversely, learning-based surrogates, such as neural networks [10] or
Gaussian processes [43], introduce severe surrogate training overhead and risk
model misspecification in extreme p ≫n regimes.
2.5. Unique Positioning and Structural Advancements of KDS-EL
To overcome the collective limitations identified in the existing literature,
KDS-EL establishes a closed-loop, knowledge-guided evolutionary learning
framework that unifies offline domain priors, online memory harvesting, and
stage-aware objective scheduling:
1. Soft filter-wrapper seeding vs. hard truncation: Unlike conventional
filter wrappers that permanently eliminate features via hard truncation,
KDS-EL uses normalized MI scores as continuous Gaussian-perturbed
seeds, preserving the full search space while ensuring an informative,
sparse starting basin with ∥x∥0 ≪D/2.
2. Online cumulative knowledge distillation vs. memoryless pertur-
bation: In contrast to black-box swarm mutations, KDS-EL dynami-
cally harvests evolutionary fitness feedback into a cumulative impor-
tance matrix W, regulated by an exponential forgetting factor γ = 0.99.
The KER operator re-deploys this accumulated knowledge to execute
targeted, directional elite flips within a strictly bounded computational
budget with ke = 3 ≪N.
3. Dynamic stage-aware objective scheduling vs. static weights:

4. Proposed KDS-EL Algorithm
In this section, we present the comprehensive architectural design, math-
ematical formulation, and algorithmic execution of the proposed Knowledge-
Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning (KDS-EL) algorithm. KDS-
EL is designed specifically to overcome three fundamental structural deficien-
cies of conventional particle swarm optimization in ultra-high-dimensional fea-
ture selection: (i) the search inefficiency and wasted evaluation budget caused
by uniform random initialization in sparse feature spaces, (ii) the loss of his-
torical cross-iteration feature-importance signals due to memoryless random
perturbation, and (iii) the inability of static objective weighting to adapt to the
divergent requirements of coarse feature reduction versus fine-grained accuracy
refinement.
4.1. Overall Algorithmic Architecture
The macroscopic architecture of KDS-EL is illustrated in Fig. 1. Built upon
a cooperative multi-task dual-subpopulation backbone [12], KDS-EL divides
a population of N = 20 binary particles into two collaborative subpopula-
tions of equal size (N1 = N2 = 10). Subpopulation 1 (P1) executes standard
continuous-space PSO velocity-position updates with an inter-task knowledge
transfer channel directing particles toward the historical global best of Subpop-
ulation 2. Subpopulation 2 (P2) performs probability-driven feature sampling
guided by the population’s accumulated feature-importance landscape.
The optimization process operates in a closed loop through four tightly
coupled functional stages:
1. Filter-level quality bootstrapping (offline): Mutual information (MI)
between each feature and class labels is computed once prior to evolu-
tion, establishing a normalized prior relevance vector m ∈[0, 1]D.
2. Knowledge-informed hybrid seeding: An initial population of N par-
ticles is generated by blending Gaussian-perturbed MI seeds with uni-
form random particles at a ratio of MI_init_ratio = 0.50, followed by
Heaviside step binarization with threshold θ = 0.60.
3. Dynamic two-phase evolutionary loop: Across T = 100 iterations,
the objective weights w(t) = [wa(t), wf (t)] dynamically transition from
aggressive feature pruning (w1 = [0.70, 0.30] for t ≤0.7T) to accuracy-
oriented refinement (w2 = [0.95, 0.05] for t > 0.7T).
4. Accumulative knowledge harvesting and elite refinement: Fitness
improvements during evaluation are harvested into a continuous
feature-importance matrix W ∈RD, discounted by an exponential
forgetting factor γ = 0.99. The top elite_k = 3 elite individuals are
refined via the KER operator and admitted via 1-to-1 in-place replace-
ment. An adaptive re-initialization mechanism monitors stagnation and
re-seeds the lower half of the population if global-best fitness stalls for
stall_patience = 20 generations.

5. Experimental Results and Analysis
This section reports the experimental outcomes across six complementary
perspectives: overall classification accuracy, feature-selection compactness, sta-
tistical significance, ceiling-effect behavior, convergence dynamics, and com-
putational efficiency. All aggregate numbers are computed over the 20 bench-
mark datasets described in Section 5.1. We then quantify the contribution of

5.1. Experimental Setup
This subsection outlines the experimental framework designed to rigor-
ously assess the performance, compactness, scalability, and robustness of KDS-
EL. To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we establish a standardized testbed
spanning benchmark datasets across diverse domains, state-of-the-art compar-
ison algorithms, uniform experimental configurations, and a formal statistical
validation protocol.
5.1.1. Benchmark Datasets and Domain Characteristics
The empirical evaluation is conducted on 20 widely recognized bench-
mark datasets drawn from five distinct real-world domains: 15 biomedical gene-
expression microarrays (ALL1, ALL4, ALL_AML, Adenoma, Colon, DLBCL,
Gastric, Gastric1, Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, Myeloma, Prostate, SR-
BCT, and T1D), two text categorization corpora (BASEHOCK and PCMAC),
one computer vision image dataset (COIL20), one spoken speech dataset (ISO-
LET), and one synthetic classification benchmark (madelon). The comprehen-
sive characteristics of these datasets are summarized in Table 3.
The benchmark suite embodies several critical challenges inherent to high-
dimensional feature selection:
1. Extreme dimensionality and the p ≫n bottleneck: The feature di-
mensionality D ranges from 500 (madelon) to 54,675 (T1D), while in-
stance counts Nsamp vary between 36 (Adenoma) and 2,600 (madelon).
Crucially, 17 out of the 20 datasets exhibit the extreme p ≫n regime
where the dimensionality exceeds the sample size by several orders
of magnitude, making conventional machine learning models severely
prone to overfitting and the curse of dimensionality.
2. Diverse class structures and data modalities: The collection spans
17 binary classification problems as well as multi-class tasks, includ-
ing SRBCT (4 classes), COIL20 (20 classes), and ISOLET (26 classes),
testing algorithmic generalization across continuous microarray expres-
sions, sparse text frequencies, and dense pixel representations.
3. Presence of accuracy ceiling datasets: The suite explicitly includes
eight gene-expression datasets—ALL1, ALL_AML, Adenoma, DLBCL,
Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, and SRBCT—on which competitive
feature selection methods routinely achieve near-perfect classification
accuracy exceeding 0.998. On these datasets, classification accuracy
saturates, and the number of selected features serves as the primary
discriminant of algorithmic effectiveness, as analyzed in Section 5.5.
To prevent data leakage during feature relevance estimation and evaluation,
a stratified 5-fold cross-validation scheme with random shuffling is strictly en-
forced. The cross-validation partitioning is performed prior to feature selection,
ensuring that candidate subsets are evaluated strictly on validation folds.
5.1.2. Comparison Algorithms and Methodological Categorization
To evaluate KDS-EL comprehensively against modern evolutionary fea-
ture selection paradigms, 14 fixed-length methods are compared under iden-
tical evaluation budgets. As categorized in Table 2, the competitor suite en-
compasses: (i) the proposed framework and its systematically stripped ablative
configurations (KDS-EL, KDS-EL w/o TPS, KDS-EL w/o TPS&KER, and

gorithms (FWPSO [8], PSO-CSM [11], SaWDE [9], IGPSO [10], and classical

and MOPSO-FS [32]). All comparison algorithms adopt the hyper-parameter
settings recommended in their original publications.
5.1.3. Parameter Configurations and Experimental Environment
To guarantee fairness across all comparative experiments, a uniform ex-
perimental protocol is applied. The k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) classifier with
k = 3 serves as the standard evaluation model due to its non-parametric na-
ture and sensitivity to feature subset quality. Each algorithm is executed with
a population size of N = 20 candidate solutions over T = 100 iterations. For
continuous-to-binary transformation in continuous velocity-based algorithms,
the standard threshold θ = 0.60 is employed. Every experiment is repeated over
10 independent runs with distinct random seeds.
The hyper-parameters for KDS-EL are configured as follows: knowledge

tialization ratio MI_init_ratio = 0.50; and stagnation patience stall_patience =
20 generations. Table 4 provides the complete summary of parameter settings.
All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core
i5-13700KF CPU @ 3.40 GHz and 32 GB RAM under Windows 11 and MAT-

Statistical significance testing protocol: To verify whether observed im-
provements are statistically meaningful, we adhere to the multi-dataset statisti-

1. Friedman Mean Rank Test [46]: Computed across the 20 × 14 accu-
racy matrix to determine global algorithm rankings (lower rank indi-

5.4. Statistical Significance and Hypothesis Testing
To rigorously substantiate whether the empirical superiority of KDS-EL
is statistically significant rather than an artifact of random sampling variation,
we apply the comprehensive statistical evaluation framework established by
Demšar [45].
5.4.1. Pairwise Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests
We perform pairwise one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at the α =
0.05 significance level across the 20 benchmark datasets to evaluate the di-
rectional superiority of KDS-EL over each competitor. Table 7 summarizes the
win/loss/tie outcomes.
The statistical results demonstrate that:
1. KDS-EL significantly outperforms MEL, PSO-CSM, IGPSO, and
BPSO on all 20 benchmark datasets without any loss.
2. KDS-EL significantly outperforms KDS-EL w/o TPS&KER, FWPSO,
and SaWDE on 19 out of 20 datasets.
3. Compared with its immediate ablative counterpart, KDS-EL w/o TPS,
KDS-EL achieves 13 wins and 7 ties across the 20 datasets, confirming
the statistically significant advantage of Two-Phase Weight Scheduling.
4. Against the five multi-objective methods, KDS-EL achieves 14 to 18
wins across the 20 datasets (14 against W-MOPSO-FS, 15 against
fMOPSO-FS, 16 against NSGA-II-FS and MOEA-D-FS, and 18

5.5. Ceiling-Effect Analysis on Biomedical Gene-Expression Datasets
A critical empirical phenomenon observed in high-dimensional biomed-
ical feature selection is the accuracy ceiling effect. Among the 20 benchmark
datasets, eight gene-expression microarrays—ALL1, ALL_AML, Adenoma, DL-
BCL, Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, and SRBCT—exhibit classification ac-
curacy exceeding 0.998 across top-performing methods.
Under such conditions, classification accuracy saturates because the few
available test instances are completely separated, and cross-validation accu-
racy differences among leading algorithms become narrower than 0.002, cor-
responding to stochastic variation in at most one sample. Consequently, clas-
sification accuracy ceases to provide discriminative utility, and the number of
selected features ( ¯d) becomes the sole meaningful criterion for assessing algo-
rithmic quality.
On these eight ceiling datasets, KDS-EL achieves an average of only 39 to
83 selected features, such as 39 features on ALL1, 45 on Lymphoma, and 64 on
DLBCL. In stark contrast:
1. Single-objective FWPSO requires 278 to 1,362 features to achieve com-
parable accuracy.
2. Multi-objective methods retain between 700 and 10,701 features on the
same datasets, where MOPSO-FS selects 5,920 features on ALL1 and
10,701 features on Gastric2.
3. Standard MEL selects 2,483 to 4,248 features across these datasets.
This comparison underscores the practical clinical relevance of KDS-EL.
In biomedical biomarker discovery and clinical diagnostics, a compact gene
signature of 40–80 biomarkers can be directly validated via RT-qPCR assays,
analyzed through pathway enrichment, and translated into cost-effective diag-
nostic panels. Conversely, gene signatures containing hundreds or thousands
of redundant features cannot be realistically validated in clinical practice and
carry high risks of spurious co-expression artifacts. Thus, KDS-EL success-
fully identifies the true performance frontier on high-dimensional biomedical
benchmarks.

5.9.1. Parametric Dynamics and Mechanism-Level Insights
The experimental trajectories across the five hyper-parameters yield crucial
insights into the optimization behavior of KDS-EL:
1. Knowledge weight decay factor (γ): This parameter controls the ex-
ponential discounting rate of historical feature-importance scores in
matrix W. At the default setting of γ = 0.99, the four-dataset aggre-
gate yields 0.944 accuracy, 107 features, and 51.3 s runtime. Setting
γ too low (such as γ = 0.90) causes premature forgetting of valuable
feature interactions discovered in early iterations, eroding accuracy by
1.2 pp to 0.932. Conversely, disabling decay entirely (γ = 1.00) ac-
cumulates stale historical evidence without discounting, inflating the
average feature count from 107 to 151 features without accuracy gains.
Performance remains exceptionally stable across the broad band γ ∈
[0.95, 0.995], with accuracy fluctuations remaining under ±1.2 pp.
2. Phase split ratio (α): This parameter dictates the temporal transition
boundary between Phase 1 feature reduction and Phase 2 accuracy re-
finement. Setting α < 0.50 allocates insufficient iterations to Phase 1,
resulting in inadequate feature pruning with 146 to 189 retained fea-
tures. Conversely, an overly prolonged Phase 1 (α = 0.90) severely
starves the Phase 2 accuracy refinement window, causing a 0.7 pp drop
in accuracy. The broad interval α ∈[0.60, 0.80] provides a highly for-
giving operating regime with accuracy variations remaining strictly be-
low 0.5 pp, establishing α = 0.70 as an optimal default.
3. KER perturbation fraction (ϕ): This parameter governs the fraction of
dimensions perturbed during knowledge-guided elite refinement. At the
default ϕ = 0.01, corresponding to flipping approximately one feature
per 100 dimensions, KER maintains an ideal balance between local-
ized exploitation and exploratory perturbation. Decreasing ϕ to 0.005
overly restricts elite exploration, slowing convergence by 3.5 s. Increas-
ing ϕ ≥0.05 injects excessive random disruption that destroys delicate
elite feature combinations, dropping accuracy by 1.3 pp and doubling
the feature count to 203. The narrow, effective window ϕ ∈[0.005, 0.02]
confirms the necessity of localized elite refinement.
4. MI initialization ratio (ρ): As the primary bootstrap mechanism, ρ
exhibits the highest individual sensitivity among all tested parameters.
Completely disabling MI seeding (ρ = 0.0, corresponding to uniform
random initialization) forces unguided early exploration, resulting in a
1.2 pp accuracy penalty and a 21% increase in computational runtime to
62.3 s as particles must rediscover informative subspaces from scratch.
Conversely, initializing 100% of the swarm with MI seeds (ρ = 1.0)
homogenizes the initial population and induces premature convergence,
causing a 0.8 pp drop in accuracy. The default balanced ratio ρ = 0.50
perfectly reconciles prior domain knowledge injection with stochastic
search diversity, maintaining robust performance across the wide band
ρ ∈[0.25, 0.75].
5. Elite pool size (ke): With N = 20, ke = 3 allocates 15% of the swarm
to elite refinement. Refining only a single elite (ke = 1) restricts knowl-
edge propagation across subpopulations, yielding a lower accuracy of
0.938, whereas selecting too many elites (ke = 10) dilutes the quality
signal of matrix W, yielding 0.935 accuracy with 141 features. The sta-
ble range ke ∈[2, 5] consistently achieves accuracy within 0.3 pp of the
optimum.
5.9.2. Plug-and-Play Generalization
Across all five hyper-parameters, the maximum accuracy deviation from
the default setting never exceeds ±1.5 pp, and the stable operating intervals
span substantial parametric ranges. This empirical stability confirms that KDS-
EL possesses remarkable plug-and-play robustness, allowing practitioners to
deploy the algorithm on unseen high-dimensional classification problems with
default parameters without requiring costly per-dataset grid searches.
6. Conclusion
This paper presented Knowledge-Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolution-
ary Learning (KDS-EL), a closed-loop evolutionary feature selection frame-
work for high-dimensional classification tasks. KDS-EL synergistically inte-
grates three core mechanisms into a cooperative multi-task PSO architecture: (i)
mutual-information-guided population initialization with adaptive re-injection
to establish an informative search basin and prevent premature stagnation; (ii)
Knowledge-guided Elite Refinement (KER) that dynamically harvests cross-
iteration feature-importance feedback with exponential memory decay to direct
elite solutions; and (iii) Two-Phase Weight Scheduling (TPS) that temporally
decouples early aggressive feature pruning from late fine accuracy refinement.
Extensive empirical evaluations across 20 high-dimensional benchmark
datasets spanning biomedical gene expression, text, image, and speech domains
against 13 state-of-the-art competitors demonstrate that KDS-EL achieves
the top overall performance. KDS-EL secures a mean classification accuracy
of 0.9574 with an average of only 87.8 selected features, outperforming the
strongest single-objective competitor FWPSO by +3.97 pp and the strongest
multi-objective competitor W-MOPSO-FS by +4.42 pp, while reducing

Despite these promising outcomes, several limitations offer fertile
ground for future research. First, the mutual information estimation entails an
O(D log D) preprocessing complexity; while negligible for moderate dimen-

omics data exceeding 106 features, where GPU-accelerated parallel estimation
or randomized sub-sampling could be explored. Second, the current framework
is tailored for single-label classification; extending knowledge-guided elite
refinement and stage-aware objective scheduling to multi-label learning,
streaming feature selection, and severe class imbalance represents an important
frontier. Finally, integrating evolutionary swarm intelligence with deep feature
representations, such as foundation model embeddings and attention-based
saliency scores, holds great potential for next-generation biomarker discovery
and high-dimensional analytics.
