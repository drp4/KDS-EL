Abstract
Feature selection in high-dimensional data, where the number of features vastly exceeds the number of samples, remains a central
challenge for evolutionary computation. Generic particle swarm optimization (PSO) suffers from three structural deficiencies in
this setting: random initialization wastes search budget in sparse solution spaces, uniform mutation discards cross-iteration feature-
importance signals, and fixed objective weights are misaligned with the distinct phases of the search. This paper proposes KDS-EL,
a knowledge-guided evolutionary learning method that builds upon a multi-task PSO backbone and integrates three complementary
mechanisms. Mutual-information-guided initialization with adaptive re-initialization supplies informed starting points and escapes
local optima on stagnation. A Knowledge-guided Elite Refinement (KER) strategy accumulates a cross-iteration feature-weight
matrix governed by exponential decay and flips elite solutions toward historically effective features at zero additional classifier
evaluations. A Two-Phase Weight Scheduling strategy partitions the search into an aggressive compression phase followed by
an accuracy-refinement phase. On 20 high-dimensional benchmark datasets spanning gene expression, text, image, and speech
domains, KDS-EL is compared against 13 competitors, including five single-objective PSO variants and five multi-objective feature
selection methods. KDS-EL ranks first with a mean accuracy of 0.9574 and only 87.8 selected features on average, outperforming
the strongest single-objective and multi-objective competitors by +3.97 and +4.42 percentage points, respectively, while delivering
a 3.4× speedup over the MEL backbone. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm superiority over MEL on 20/20 datasets, and Holm­
Bonferroni correction establishes statistical significance against all 13 competitors. The component contribution analysis shows that
the three mechanisms yield cumulative gains of +7.06 percentage points in accuracy and compress the feature count from 2,722 to
88.

1. Introduction
High-dimensional data now pervades science and engi-
neering, from gene expression microarrays to text-mining and
computer-vision representations. In these domains the number
of measured features routinely reaches tens of thousands
while labeled instances rarely exceed a few hundred, a regime
denoted p  n. Under this condition, standard learning
algorithms overfit, computational overhead escalates, and
model interpretability degrades--the curse of dimensional-
ity [1, 2]. Feature selection (FS), the task of identifying a
compact subset of informative features before model training,
defends against these effects by eliminating redundant and
irrelevant variables [1, 2]. Despite decades of development,
high-dimensional FS remains an open challenge: combinatorial
growth of the search space precludes exhaustive enumeration,
while pervasive feature correlations confound simple marginal
screening.
Feature selection methods fall into three paradigms. Fil-
ter methods rank features by statistical scores such as mutual

2. Related Work
2.1. Feature Selection Paradigms
Feature selection is a fundamental pre-processing step in
high-dimensional data analysis, aiming to identify a minimal
subset of relevant features that preserves or improves classifi-
cation performance while reducing computational cost and en-
hancing interpretability [1, 2]. Methods fall broadly into three
paradigms: filter, wrapper, and embedded.
Filter methods evaluate features independently of any
learning algorithm, relying on intrinsic data properties such
as variance, correlation, or eigenvector centrality [8, 9].
Representative techniques include the minimum Redundancy
Maximum Relevance (mRMR) criterion [10] and the Fast
Correlation-Based Filter (FCBF) [11]. These methods are
computationally efficient and scale readily to high-dimensional
settings, yet they disregard feature interactions and may retain
redundant subsets under strong inter-feature correlation.
Wrapper methods treat feature selection as a search
problem, evaluating candidate subsets by the predictive perfor-
mance of a specific classifier [3]. Hybrid genetic algorithms
[12] and other meta-heuristic wrappers can capture complex
feature interdependencies, but their computational cost grows
rapidly with dimensionality, as each candidate subset requires
a full classifier training-and-evaluation cycle.
Embedded methods integrate feature selection into model
training. Lasso and its bootstrapped variant Bolasso [13]
enforce sparsity through L1
regularization; gradient-boosting-
based approaches such as FS-GBDT [14] leverage tree-split
importance; and hybrid algorithms like ILRC [15] combine
regularization with clustering. Embedded methods offer a
favorable speed­accuracy trade-off, but their feature rankings
are tied to specific model assumptions.
At high dimensionality, each paradigm faces distinct struc-
tural bottlenecks: filters are fast but oblivious to feature interac-
tions, wrappers are accurate but face a search space that grows
2
exponentially with dimension, and embedded methods depend
on model-specific regularization biases. These complementary
weaknesses motivate evolutionary computation, which decou-
ples the search mechanism from the evaluation metric and navi-
gates the exploration­exploitation trade-off through population-
based global search.
2.2. Single-Objective Evolutionary Feature Selection
Evolutionary algorithms have been extensively applied
to feature selection for their population-based global search
and gradient-free nature. Early work adapted classical meta-
heuristics--binary PSO (BPSO) [16], ant colony optimization
(ACO) [17], artificial bee colony (ABC) [18], and genetic
algorithms with support vector machines (GA-SVM) [19]--to
the binary subset selection problem, establishing the template
of a population of candidate subsets evolving under selection,
crossover, and mutation guided by a scalar fitness function.
Because standard evolutionary operators degrade at high
dimensionality, specialized single-objective methods have been
developed: self-adaptive PSO adjusting swarm size and muta-
tion rate [20], progressively refined PSO alternating between
coarse- and fine-grained search [21], and problem-specific
heuristics--FWPSO [22], which biases velocity updates with
per-feature importance; SaWDE [23] with adaptive weighting;
IGPSO [24], which estimates relevance via a multi-layer
perceptron; and PSO-CSM [25] integrating multiple evaluation
criteria. Learning-based control and space decomposition
are also explored: reinforcement-learning-aided PSO [26]
and divide-and-conquer search [27]. These methods refine
search operators but do not systematically exploit accumulated
feature-importance knowledge harvested from the search itself.
2.3. Multi-Objective Evolutionary Feature Selection
Multi-objective formulations treat classification accuracy
and feature count as competing objectives, producing a Pareto
front of non-dominated solutions. Xue et al. [28] pioneered
multi-objective PSO for feature selection, showing that jointly
optimizing accuracy and subset size yields more diverse and
informative solutions than scalarized approaches. Hu et al.
[29] extended this framework with fuzzy cost functions for
imbalanced classification.
The multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (MOEA)
family provides several architectures for this problem.
NSGA-II-FS [30] uses non-dominated sorting and crowding
distance to maintain Pareto-front diversity. MOEA/D-FS
[31] decomposes the problem into scalar subproblems solved
cooperatively. MOPSO-FS [32] extends single-objective
PSO with an external archive and Pareto-dominance-based
leader selection. For high-dimensional data, Cheng et al. [33]
proposed a variable-granularity search method (VGS-MOEA)
that transitions from coarse to fine-grained search, and Hancer
et al. [34] combined evolutionary search with filter-based
ranking under both single- and multi-objective settings.
Despite their conceptual advantages, MOFS methods in-
cur substantial overhead: maintaining an external archive, non-
dominated sorting, and Pareto-front management all cost signif-
icant time (in our experiments, over 110 seconds on average).
Moreover, under fixed-length binary encoding at high dimen-
sionality, the Pareto front expands combinatorially and conver-
gence slows, limiting scalability at gene-expression scale.
2.4. Evolutionary Multi-Task Learning
Evolutionary multi-task learning (EMTL) solves multiple
related tasks jointly, with knowledge transfer accelerating con-
vergence. Zhang and Yang [35] surveyed its foundations, and
several instantiations target feature selection: a minion-based
multitasking framework [36], PSO-based multitasking for high-
dimensional classification [37, 38], multitask bee colony opti-
mization for hyperspectral band selection [39], and multitask-
ing for large-scale multi-objective optimization [40].
Of particular relevance, Wang et al. [7] proposed MEL
(Multi-task Evolutionary Learning), a dual-swarm multi-task
PSO in which one swarm optimizes binary feature subsets while
a second learns continuous feature weights, with the swarms
cooperating through cross-task knowledge transfer. MEL was
selected as the backbone for three reasons: its fixed-length en-
coding is efficient at high dimensionality and naturally compat-
ible with incremental augmentation; its dual-space cooperation
provides a built-in mechanism for capturing feature relevance
signals; and it has demonstrated strong performance on gene-
expression benchmarks.
Nevertheless, MEL exhibits three structural limitations.
Its population is initialized entirely at random, wasting search
budget in the sparse high-dimensional space. Its operators--
velocity update and mutation--are uniform and stochastic,
never incorporating cross-iteration knowledge about which
features have proven beneficial. And its dual-objective weights
remain fixed throughout search, imposing a rigid schedule that
cannot adapt to the natural phasing of feature selection, where
early iterations benefit from aggressive compression and later
iterations demand fine-grained accuracy refinement.
2.5. Gene-Expression Feature Selection
High-dimensional gene expression data present a partic-
ularly challenging testbed: the number of features routinely
exceeds the number of samples by three to four orders of
magnitude--a "small N, large D" regime where conventional
statistical methods break down and overfitting is pervasive
[41, 42]. Representative applications include cancer staging
[43], Alzheimer's classification from neuroimaging features
[44], biomarker gene identification [22], and multi-cancer risk
module discovery [14, 15].
Two structural properties amplify the difficulty. First, fea-
ture redundancy is exceptionally high: co-regulated genes ex-
hibit correlated expression, so distinct subsets frequently yield
near-identical accuracy. Second, a non-trivial fraction of gene
datasets reach accuracy ceilings--exceeding 0.998 under mul-
tiple methods--rendering accuracy alone insufficient to distin-
guish algorithmic quality; on these datasets the selected feature
count becomes the primary discriminatory metric. Evaluation
must therefore jointly consider classification accuracy and sub-
set compactness.

2.6. Positioning of KDS-EL
KDS-EL builds upon the multi-task PSO backbone of MEL,
adding three mechanisms that each target one structural limita-
tion: MI-guided initialization with adaptive re-initialization ad-
dresses the cold-start problem; the KER strategy injects cross-
iteration feature importance into elite refinement at no addi-
tional cost; and Two-Phase Weight Scheduling replaces static
weights with a compression-then-refinement schedule. These
mechanisms preserve the fixed-length encoding that makes the
backbone suitable for high-dimensional applications.

4. Proposed KDS-EL Algorithm
4.1. Overall Framework
KDS-EL is a single-stream, pool-free evolutionary
algorithm integrating three complementary mechanisms
within a multi-task particle swarm optimization backbone.
Figure 1 shows the complete pipeline transforming raw high-
dimensional data into a compact, high-accuracy feature subset
through knowledge-guided operations.
The algorithm takes as input a dataset with D features
and C classes. In the preprocessing stage, mutual information
between each feature and the class label is computed to
produce a quality ranking over all D dimensions (Section 4.2).
An initial population of N fixed-length binary individuals is
then constructed by mixing MI-ranked seeds with randomly
initialized individuals at a controlled ratio MI_init_ratio = 0.5,
followed by sigmoid binarization at threshold  = 0.6. This
hybrid scheme supplies a quality-informed starting point while
preserving the diversity necessary for effective exploration.
The population enters the main iterative loop. Each
generation opens with dual-objective fitness evaluation under
the Two-Phase Weight Scheduling weights (Section 4.5).
Observed improvements update the cumulative feature-weight
matrix W  RD with exponential decay  = 0.99 (Section 4.3).
The top elite_k = 3 individuals are refined by the KER strategy
(Section 4.4)--a zero-extra-evaluation flip mechanism with a
90%/10% knowledge-to-random split--then undergo the stan-
dard PSO update and replace their pre-refinement counterparts
in-place, avoiding the 2N-candidate pool of earlier designs.
On stagnation for stall_patience = 20 generations, adaptive
MI re-initialization refreshes the lower-performing half of the
population. After T generations, the best individual is decoded
as the final feature subset.
KDS-EL builds upon the multi-task evolutionary learn-
ing framework of [7], inheriting its fixed-length binary
encoding and co-evolution mechanism while adding three
knowledge-driven components. These operate at indepen-
dent layers--feature-quality input (Section 4.2), knowledge
accumulation and exploitation (Sections 4.3 and 4.4), and
objective scheduling (Section 4.5)--so each contribution is
independently verifiable.

4.5. Two-Phase Weight Scheduling Strategy
Conventional PSO-based feature selection methods employ
fixed objective weights throughout the entire search, typically
balancing classification error and feature ratio with a static
weighting such as w = [0.9, 0.1]. This rigid scheduling fails to
exploit a structural property of the feature selection problem:
noise-feature elimination is a one-time cost best handled ag-
gressively at the outset, whereas accuracy refinement benefits
from sustained fine-tuning over a compact, already-pruned
feature set. A single fixed weight vector must compromise
between these two temporally distinct demands.

4.7. Complexity Analysis
The computational cost of KDS-EL decomposes into a one-
time initialization cost and per-iteration costs. The initial MI
ranking requires computing I( fj
; Y) for all D features and sort-
ing the scores, costing O(D log D). Each generation incurs:
(a) fitness evaluation over N individuals with KNN classifica-
tion, O(N · Teval
), where Teval
dominates all other per-iteration
operations; (b) weight-matrix update across Pbest-improving
individuals, O(N · D); (c) elite selection and KER refinement
(sorting D weights plus flipping D features for elite_k indi-
viduals), O(elite_k · D). The adaptive MI re-initialization ex-
ecutes only on stagnation, contributing an amortized O(D) per
iteration. Collecting terms, the total complexity is:
O D log D + T · (N · Teval
+ N · D + elite_k · D) . (10)
The baseline MEL [7] has complexity O(T · N · Teval
). The

to O(N · Teval

51.3 s versus 175.0 s for MEL (3.4× speedup), confirming that
improved initialization and faster convergence more than offset
the marginal overhead.
5. Experimental Setup
This section details the experimental framework. The
evaluation employs 20 benchmark datasets drawn from five
domains: 15 gene expression microarrays (ALL1, ALL4,
ALL_AML, Adenoma, Colon, DLBCL, Gastric, Gastric1,
Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, Myeloma, Prostate, SRBCT,
and T1D), two text corpora (BASEHOCK and PCMAC), one
image dataset (COIL20), one speech dataset (ISOLET), and
one synthetic dataset (madelon). Table 2 summarizes their
key characteristics. The datasets span feature dimensionalities
from 500 (madelon) to 54,675 (T1D) and sample sizes from
36 (Adenoma) to 2,600 (madelon), capturing the hallmark
p  n configuration: 18 of 20 datasets possess more features
than instances. Class structure ranges from 17 binary problems
to SRBCT (4 classes), COIL20 (20), and ISOLET (26). The
collection was selected to span low-, medium-, and ultra-high-
dimensional regimes and to include eight gene-expression
datasets--ALL1, ALL_AML, Adenoma, DLBCL, Gastric2,
Leukaemia, Lymphoma, and SRBCT--on which methods
routinely reach near-perfect accuracy (above 0.998). On these
ceiling datasets accuracy alone ceases to discriminate among
methods, so the number of selected features becomes the
decisive metric; Section 6.4 pursues this evaluation.
Fourteen fixed-length feature selection methods are com-
pared, organized into three groups. The first comprises progres-
sive variants of the proposed framework: the multi-task back-
bone MEL [7], the MI-augmented variant MEL+MI, and KDS-
ELKER
, which adds the KER strategy while omitting two-phase
scheduling. The second consists of five single-objective PSO-
based methods: FWPSO [22], PSO-CSM [25], SaWDE [23],
IGPSO [24], and the classic BPSO [16]. The third contains five
multi-objective feature selection (MOFS) methods jointly op-
timizing accuracy and subset size: W-MOPSO-FS (a self-built
Wrapper­Filter hybrid baseline), fMOPSO-FS [48], NSGA-II-
FS [30], MOEA-D-FS [31], and MOPSO-FS [32]. Table 1
maps methods to references. All comparison methods use the
hyper-parameters recommended in their original publications,
retaining default values where a parameter is unspecified.
All methods share a unified configuration. A K-nearest
neighbor (KNN) classifier with k = 3 serves as the base
evaluator, with accuracy estimated via stratified 5-fold
cross-validation with random shuffling. Each PSO-based
algorithm uses N = 20 particles and T = 100 iterations,
and the continuous-to-binary mapping threshold is  = 0.6.
Every experiment is repeated across 10 independent runs
with distinct random seeds. The KDS-EL parameter set is:
knowledge weight decay WeightDecay = 0.99; phase split
PhaseSplit = 0.7 with objective weights ws
= [0.7, 0.3]
(Phase 1, compression) and ws
= [0.95, 0.05] (Phase 2,
refinement); KER flip fraction flip_frac = 0.01; random
exploration probability rand_flip_p = 0.10; MI-init ratio
MI_init_ratio = 0.5; elite count elite_k = 3; and stall patience
stall_patience = 20 for adaptive MI re-initialization. Table 3
summarizes these settings. Experiments run on a desktop
workstation with an Intel i5-13700KF CPU and 32 GB RAM.
For each method and dataset we report classification
accuracy (mean and standard deviation over 10 runs), the
number of selected features with its selection ratio, and runtime
in seconds. Statistical significance follows the protocol of
Demsar [49]: the Friedman rank test [50] on the 20-dataset
accuracy matrix detects overall differences; pairwise Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests (p < 0.05) compare KDS-EL with each of the
13 methods; and the resulting p-values are Holm­Bonferroni
corrected [51] to control the family-wise error rate. This
standard protocol establishes whether observed differences
are statistically meaningful rather than artifacts of finite
experimental repetition.

6.2. Feature-Selection Compactness
KDS-EL selects, on average, 87.8 features across the
20-dataset benchmark, corresponding to an effective selection
ratio of approximately 2­3%. For comparison, the original
MEL backbone retains 2,722 features, and all multi-objective
methods (W-MOPSO-FS, fMOPSO-FS, NSGA-II-FS, MOEA-
D-FS, MOPSO-FS) select between 2,960 and 5,263 features
on average. Even the most compact single-objective com-
petitor, FWPSO, selects 1,069 features--more than an order
of magnitude above KDS-EL. Fig. 3 reports the per-dataset
selection ratio of all 14 methods on a log scale, underscoring
the consistent compactness of KDS-EL relative to every
competitor.
This combined finding--equal or higher accuracy with
20- to 50-fold compression--has a clear Pareto interpretation:
in the accuracy-versus-features plane, KDS-EL occupies the
extreme top-left region no competing method can dominate.
The compression is most pronounced on gene-expression data:
on ALL1, 0.9998 accuracy with 39 features (0.31% of 12,625)

multi-objective method; on Colon, 14 features--a 140:1

6.4. Ceiling-Effect Analysis on Gene-Expression Data
Eight datasets in the benchmark--ALL1, ALL_AML,
Adenoma, DLBCL, Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, and
SRBCT--exhibit mean accuracy at or above 0.998 across
the top-performing methods. On these datasets, the range
of accuracy differences among the leading 4­5 methods is
narrower than 0.002, which corresponds to at most one or
two misclassified instances across ten independent five-fold
cross-validation runs. At this saturation level, classification
accuracy ceases to be a meaningful discriminator; instead, the
number of features selected becomes the primary figure of
merit.
KDS-EL selects between 39 (ALL1) and 83 (Gastric2)
features across these eight datasets. In contrast, the best
single-objective competitor, FWPSO, retains 278­1,362 fea-
tures, and the multi-objective methods select between 700
and over 3,400 features on the same data. This gap carries
practical significance for gene-expression analysis: a compact
signature of 40­80 genes is amenable to targeted biological
validation, pathway enrichment analysis, and potential clinical
deployment, whereas signatures spanning several hundred
to several thousand genes offer little interpretability and are
vulnerable to overfitting in the p  n regime.
That KDS-EL ties or marginally trails methods achieving
perfect 1.0 on ALL1 and SRBCT does not indicate inferior dis-
crimination: all methods misclassify fewer than two samples
on average, differing only in which samples are misclassified--
stochastic variation expected in small-sample data. A similar
pattern holds on Lymphoma (45 samples), where KDS-EL and
KDS-ELKER
share the best accuracy of 0.9994 with a difference
of zero, yet KDS-EL achieves this with 45 features versus 884
for W-MOPSO-FS. We therefore interpret the subset of ceil-
ing datasets as evidence that KDS-EL attains the performance
frontier while employing a substantially leaner feature repre-
sentation.

6.6. Computational Efficiency
KDS-EL records a mean runtime of 51.3 seconds, ranking
second among all methods and trailing FWPSO by only
1.6 seconds (49.7 s). Relative to the original MEL backbone
(175.0 s), KDS-EL achieves a 3.4× speedup. All five multi-
objective methods exceed 110 seconds on average--more than
double the runtime of KDS-EL--owing to the overhead of
non-dominated sorting and Pareto-archive maintenance at each
generation. Fig. 6 locates each method in the accuracy­runtime
plane, confirming that KDS-EL occupies the high-accuracy,
low-runtime corner; the complete per-dataset runtime table
appears in the Supplementary Material.
The efficiency advantage is most pronounced at high dimen-
sionality: on T1D (54,675 features) KDS-EL finishes in 118.8 s
versus 256.6 s for PSO-CSM (2.2×), and on BASEHOCK it
is 4.7× faster than SaWDE (130.1 s vs. 616.0 s). Three fac-
tors explain this: MI-guided initialization shortens the path to
a high-quality region; KER refines elites without extra KNN
evaluations; and Two-Phase scheduling concentrates compres-
sion early so search later operates on compact, cheaper subsets.
FWPSO's marginal 1.6 s lead stems from its SA-driven local
search, which terminates early on small-sample problems at the
expense of a 3.97-percentage-point accuracy deficit.

8. Parameter Sensitivity Analysis
To assess the sensitivity of KDS-EL to its key hyper-
parameters, we conduct a one-factor-at-a-time analysis on
four representative datasets: Adenoma (36 samples, 7,457 fea-
tures), Colon (62, 2,000), ALL4 (93, 12,625), and BASE-
HOCK (1,993, 4,862). The subset spans small-sample gene
expression and larger-sample text domains, with 2,000­12,625
features. Five parameters--WeightDecay, PhaseSplit, flip_frac,
MI_init_ratio, and elite_k--are varied individually while the
remaining four stay fixed at their defaults (Table 3). For
each configuration, the mean accuracy, feature count, and
runtime across the four datasets are recorded; the complete
tables appear in the Supplementary Material. Across all five
parameters, the maximum accuracy deviation from the default
configuration never exceeds ±1.5 pp.
Weight decay (WeightDecay). This parameter controls the
discounting rate of historical feature importance scores. At the
default 0.99, the four-dataset mean is 0.944 accuracy, 107 fea-
tures, and 51.3 s. Reducing the decay to 0.90 erodes accuracy
to 0.932 (-1.2 pp), as the weight matrix forgets useful patterns
too rapidly. Disabling decay (1.00) inflates the feature count
by approximately 40% (to 151) with no accuracy benefit--stale
scores accumulate without bound. Performance plateaus across
[0.95, 0.995], with accuracy fluctuation under ±1.2 pp.
Phase split ratio (PhaseSplit). This parameter governs the
proportion of iterations allocated to aggressive compression be-
fore accuracy-oriented refinement. The default 0.70 gives 70%
of iterations to Phase 1. Values below 0.50 yield insufficient
compression time, leaving elevated feature counts (146­189),
while 0.90 starves the refinement phase and reduces accuracy
by 0.7 pp. The stable band [0.60, 0.80] keeps variation below
0.5 pp, confirming the schedule tolerates imprecise split points.
Elite flip fraction (flip_frac). At the default 0.01, roughly
one feature per 100 dimensions is flipped per elite. Reducing
this to 0.005 slows convergence (runtime +3.5 s). Increasing
it beyond 0.02 injects excessive randomness: at 0.10, accuracy
drops by 1.3 pp and features nearly double (to 203), indicat-
ing random perturbation overwhelms knowledge-guided refine-
ment. Only the narrow window [0.005, 0.02] preserves the in-
tended knowledge­randomness balance.
MI initialization ratio (MI_init_ratio). This is the most
influential parameter. At the default 0.50, half the population
is seeded with MI-ranked features. Eliminating MI guidance
(0.00) imposes a cold start--accuracy falls by 1.2 pp and
runtime rises by 21% (to 62.3 s) as the search rediscovers
informative features from scratch. Full MI initialization (1.00)
causes premature convergence (-0.8 pp) through reduced
diversity. The stable band [0.25, 0.75] keeps accuracy within
0.4 pp of the optimum.
Elite count (elite_k). With N = 20, the default of three
elites represents 15% of the swarm. A single elite restricts
knowledge propagation (0.938 accuracy), while 10 elites dilute
the quality signal of the KER strategy (0.935 accuracy, 141 fea-
tures). The stable range [2, 5] maintains accuracy within 0.3 pp
of the optimum, confirming that a moderate elite subset concen-
trates computational effort on the most promising candidates
without dilution.

without per-dataset tuning; practitioners should preserve
MI_init_ratio and flip_frac, which exhibit the largest individual
sensitivity and jointly govern the balance between injected
prior knowledge and exploratory diversity.
9. Conclusion
This paper presented KDS-EL, a knowledge-guided
evolutionary learning method addressing three structural
limitations of general-purpose PSO in high-dimensional
feature selection: random initialization wastes search budget,
conventional mutation discards accumulated improvement
signals, and fixed objective weights misalign with search
phases. MI-guided initialization and adaptive re-initialization
supply informed starting points and stagnation escape (+4.58pp
over the MEL backbone [7], compressing features from 2722
to 291). The Knowledge-guided Elite Refinement (KER)
strategy accumulates cross-iteration feature weights and
conducts knowledge-guided elite flipping at zero additional
evaluation cost (+1.63pp over MEL+MI, features to 122).
Two-Phase Weight Scheduling aligns objective weights with
search phases--aggressive compression followed by accuracy
refinement--yielding gains in accuracy (+0.86pp), sparsity
(88 features), and efficiency (51.3 s). On 20 high-dimensional
datasets spanning gene expression, text, image, and speech
domains, KDS-EL ranks first among 14 fixed-length methods,
surpassing the strongest single-objective and multi-objective
competitors by +3.97pp and +4.42pp. Wilcoxon tests confirm
superiority over MEL in 20/20 datasets; Holm-Bonferroni
correction establishes significance against all 13 competitors.
KDS-EL delivers a 3.4× speedup over MEL (51.3 s vs. 175.0 s)
with parameter sensitivity within ±1.5pp.
Several limitations suggest future directions. MI-based fea-
ture ranking incurs O(D log D) preprocessing cost, a bottleneck
when D exceeds 105, where GPU-accelerated MI estimation
may be required. The method currently targets single-label
classification; extensions to multi-label learning, streaming fea-
ture selection, class-imbalanced data, and deep embedding rep-
resentations remain open. Promising directions include GPU-
accelerated MI approximation, multi-label and imbalanced ex-
tensions, transformer-based feature representations, and cross-
dataset transfer via multi-task evolutionary extensions of the
proposed framework.
