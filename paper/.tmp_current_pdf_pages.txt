A B S T R A C T
Feature selection in high-dimensional data remains a central challenge for evolutionary computation. Particle swarm
optimization suffers from three structural deficiencies: random initialization provides insufficient guidance in sparse
high-dimensional spaces, uniform feature perturbation fails to exploit cross-iteration feature-importance signals, and
static objective preferences cannot adapt to different evolutionary phases. To address these limitations, we propose
KDS-EL (Knowledge-Guided Dual-Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning), a knowledge-guided evolutionary frame-
work with a closed-loop feature-importance learning mechanism. First, to overcome inefficient initialization, we pro-
pose mutual-information-guided initialization with adaptive re-initialization, injecting prior knowledge and recovering
population diversity when stagnation occurs. Second, to exploit discarded cross-iteration feature-importance signals,
we propose a knowledge-guided elite refinement strategy that accumulates feature-importance knowledge and steers
elite solutions toward historically effective features. Third, to align objective weights with search phases, we propose
two-phase weight scheduling that emphasizes early dimensionality reduction and late-stage accuracy refinement. On
20 high-dimensional benchmarks, KDS-EL achieves the best classification performance among all competitors, with a
mean accuracy of 0.9574 and only 87.8 selected features, outperforming the strongest single- and multi-objective com-
petitors by +3.97 and +4.42 percentage points; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction confirm
statistically significant improvements over all 13 competitors.
1. Introduction
High-dimensional data have emerged as a fundamental challenge in clas-
sification problems across diverse domains, including genomics, text mining,
and computer vision. These datasets typically contain hundreds or even tens
of thousands of features, while only a limited number of labeled samples are
available, resulting in the well-known p ≫n scenario. Under such conditions,
conventional learning algorithms often suffer from severe overfitting, increased
computational complexity, and reduced interpretability due to the curse of di-
mensionality [1, 2]. Feature selection (FS) has therefore become an essential
preprocessing technique that aims to identify a compact and informative sub-
set of features while preserving classification performance. However, select-
ing an optimal feature subset remains highly challenging because the search
space grows exponentially with the number of features D, and the underlying
subset-selection problem is NP-hard [3]. Moreover, individual feature relevance
measures are insufficient to characterize complex feature interactions and de-
pendencies, which are often critical for achieving robust classification perfor-
mance.
FS methods are generally categorized into filter, wrapper, and embedded
approaches [1, 4]. Filter methods rank features according to statistical criteria
and offer high computational efficiency, but they usually evaluate features in-
dependently of the classifier and may overlook feature interactions relevant to
classification. Embedded methods incorporate feature selection into the learn-
ing process, providing a compromise between selection efficiency and predic-
tive performance; however, their effectiveness is often dependent on the char-
acteristics of specific learning models. Wrapper methods directly optimize fea-
ture subsets according to classifier performance and can achieve competitive
classification results, but the repeated model evaluations make them computa-
tionally expensive, especially in high-dimensional search spaces. To address the
combinatorial complexity of wrapper-based FS, evolutionary computation has
been extensively investigated as a promising optimization paradigm [3, 5–7].
By maintaining a population of candidate solutions, evolutionary algorithms
can perform global exploration of feature subsets without requiring gradient
information. Among them, particle swarm optimization (PSO), genetic algo-
rithms, and differential evolution have demonstrated promising results on high-
dimensional FS tasks.
PSO has become one of the most widely adopted evolutionary algorithms
for FS due to its simple structure, efficient information-sharing mechanism,
and fast convergence. Nevertheless, conventional PSO-based FS methods
still encounter three structural limitations when dealing with sparse and
high-dimensional search spaces. First, random initialization distributes

becomes more important after a compact subset has been obtained.
Recent evolutionary FS studies have attempted to improve search perfor-

tors, and refined fitness evaluation strategies [8–11]. Although these approaches
have achieved promising results, they usually focus on isolated components of
the optimization process and do not jointly address inefficient initialization, in-
sufficient exploitation of accumulated feature knowledge, and inflexible objec-
tive weighting. Recently, multi-task PSO has emerged as an effective paradigm
for high-dimensional FS with fixed-length binary representations. For instance,
MEL [12] adopts cooperative subpopulations and a fixed-length representation
to efficiently handle feature spaces containing tens of thousands of dimensions.
Despite its effectiveness, existing multi-task PSO-based FS frameworks still
leave several challenges unresolved: the initial population is generally gener-
ated without sufficient feature-level prior knowledge, useful feature-importance
information discovered during evolution is not explicitly retained and exploited,
and the balance between classification performance and feature sparsity re-
mains unchanged across different search stages.
To address these limitations, this paper proposes Knowledge-Guided Dual-
Phase Swarm Evolutionary Learning (KDS-EL), a closed-loop framework that
cohesively integrates domain prior injection, cross-iteration knowledge accu-
mulation, and dynamic objective scheduling into a cooperative multi-task PSO
backbone.

2. Related Work and Background
This section establishes the formal mathematical foundation of feature
selection and systematically reviews the relevant evolutionary computation
paradigms. Section 2.1 first formalizes the combinatorial optimization problem
and categorizes classical search paradigms. Section 2.2 subsequently analyzes
evolutionary swarm mechanics alongside the structural trade-offs between
weighted scalarization and multi-objective Pareto formulations. To address
high-dimensional search challenges, Section 2.3 scrutinizes the cooperative
multi-task evolutionary learning framework and pinpoints its operational
bottlenecks. Finally, Section 2.4 investigates knowledge-guided evolutionary
search, contrasts offline filter priors against online closed-loop memory, and
establishes the taxonomic positioning of KDS-EL in contrast to existing
literature.

such as mutual information in mRMR [20], correlation in FCBF [21], or cen-

bined. Conversely, embedded methods perform feature selection natively during
classifier training by embedding sparsity constraints into model parameter esti-
mation, such as L1-regularized regression in Bolasso [23] or tree-based ensem-
ble importance in FS-GBDT [24]. Although embedded methods bridge compu-
tational efficiency and predictive feedback, they remain architecturally bound to
specific learners and frequently encounter performance degradation under se-
vere sample scarcity where Nsamp ≪100. In contrast, wrapper methods utilize a
target learning algorithm as a black-box evaluator, assessing candidate feature
masks directly through cross-validation predictive error feedback [4, 18]. By
tightly coupling subset evaluation with classifier feedback, wrappers naturally
capture complex non-linear feature interactions and yield superior classification
accuracy.

ture selection without gradient information, Evolutionary Computation
paradigms—including Genetic Algorithms [25], Ant Colony Optimiza-
tion [26], Artificial Bee Colony [27], and Swarm Intelligence [3, 28]—have
emerged as dominant search heuristics. To combat the curse of dimensionality
in small-sample high-dimensional data, various advanced single-objective
evolutionary strategies have been introduced. For instance, self-adaptive
differential evolution with adaptive weighting (SaWDE) [9] dynamically

Because feature selection intrinsically entails two conflicting objectives—
minimizing classification error and minimizing the number of selected
features—Multi-Objective Feature Selection (MOFS) algorithms model this
trade-off through Pareto optimality. Pioneered by Xue et al. [17], diverse multi-
objective evolutionary algorithms have been tailored for feature selection,
encompassing genetic algorithms like NSGA-II-FS [13], decomposition-based
heuristics like MOEA/D-FS [14], canonical swarm methods including
MOPSO-FS [15], filter–wrapper hybrids such as W-MOPSO-FS [17], and
fuzzy Pareto-dominance techniques such as fMOPSO-FS [16]. Despite
their theoretical capability to generate a non-dominated Pareto front, MOFS
methods encounter two critical operational bottlenecks under ultra-high-
dimensional and sample-scarce regimes where D ≫103 and Nsamp ≪100.
First, maintaining an external non-dominated archive and executing Pareto
ranking over dense high-dimensional vectors incurs substantial computational
overhead, often requiring 110–140 seconds of wall-clock execution time
(Section 4.2.6). Second, under severe sample sparsity, Pareto dominance
and fuzzy ranking criteria fail to exert adequate selection pressure toward
extreme feature sparsity; in ultra-high dimensions, candidate solutions become
mutually non-dominated across irrelevant feature dimensions, populating
the Pareto front with non-sparse subsets that retain thousands of active
features rather than the compact biomarker signatures with fewer than 100
features required for clinical interpretability. To circumvent Pareto archive
bloat and achieve extreme subset sparsity within tight computational budgets,
weighted single-objective scalarization remains the prevailing formulation
in high-dimensional biomarker discovery, as exemplified by SaWDE [9],
FWPSO [8], and PSO-CSM [11]. However, existing weighted frameworks
enforce static, inflexible trade-off weights throughout the optimization process,
creating a fundamental evolutionary phase misalignment: the search process
requires aggressive feature reduction during early exploration to eliminate vast
noise subspaces, whereas delicate decision-boundary fine-tuning is required in
late-stage exploitation to maximize classification accuracy.
2.3. Multi-task evolutionary learning and the MEL framework
Evolutionary Multi-Task Optimization (EMTO) aims to solve multiple
distinct optimization tasks simultaneously by facilitating inter-task knowledge
transfer through a unified chromosomal representation [29]. In feature
selection, EMTO frameworks partition the global population into collaborative
subpopulations to explore complementary search subspaces, including minion-
assisted multi-tasking [30], evolutionary multi-task PSO for hyperspectral and
gene expression data [31, 32], multi-task bee colony band selection [33], and
large-scale multi-objective multi-tasking [34]. Most notably, Wang et al. [12]
introduced Multi-Task Evolutionary Learning (MEL), which establishes a
dual-subpopulation PSO framework. In MEL, a population of N particles
is partitioned into two collaborative subpopulations P1 and P2, with each
subpopulation comprising N/2 individuals operating on complementary search
tasks. Specifically, Subpopulation 1 executes continuous swarm exploration,
updating continuous velocities and positions using PSO dynamics augmented
with an inter-task knowledge transfer channel directing particles toward the
historical global best of Subpopulation 2 (gbest,2 ∈RD):

While MEL demonstrates the utility of cross-task knowledge transfer, its
search dynamics in ultra-high-dimensional spaces where D ≫103 suffer from
critical structural bottlenecks. Continuous particles in P1 are initialized uni-
formly across the unit hypercube, lacking domain-informed guidance toward
sparse biomarker subspaces and wasting substantial evaluation budget explor-
ing irrelevant dimensions in early iterations. Moreover, the importance matrix
WMEL accumulates historical feature frequencies unconditionally without ex-
ponential forgetting or elite fitness verification, allowing obsolete early choices
to permanently bias subsequent discrete sampling in P2 and trapping the swarm
in local optima. In addition, rigid static weighting prevents the algorithm from
dynamically adjusting its selection pressure between early aggressive feature
pruning and late high-precision boundary refinement. The proposed KDS-EL
framework directly adopts this dual-subpopulation multi-task foundation while
resolving each of these structural deficiencies through targeted mechanisms.

Existing knowledge-guided evolutionary algorithms typically adopt one-
shot offline injection via either static filter pre-filtering or static velocity bi-
asing. In static filter pre-filtering, exemplified by MI-MPODE [36] and NNS-
DFPG-CSA [19], algorithms compute feature rankings offline and initialize the
population or restrict the search space to the top-k ranked features. In static
velocity biasing, such as FWPSO [8], static feature-weight vectors are incorpo-
rated to bias particle velocity equations throughout evolution. While beneficial,
one-shot knowledge injection lacks online adaptability: once injected, the static
prior is never updated or refined by the empirical trial-and-error feedback gen-
erated during subsequent wrapper evaluations. Conversely, learning-based sur-
rogates, such as neural networks [10] or Gaussian processes [40], introduce se-
vere surrogate training overhead and risk model misspecification under sample-
scarce conditions where Nsamp ≪100.
To overcome the collective limitations identified in the existing literature,
KDS-EL establishes a closed-loop, knowledge-guided evolutionary learning
framework that unifies offline domain priors, online memory harvesting, and
stage-aware objective scheduling. Specifically, normalized mutual information
scores are embedded as continuous Gaussian-perturbed seeds, preserving
the full search space while guiding the swarm toward an ultra-sparse initial
basin, complemented by an adaptive re-initialization mechanism to recover
exploratory diversity upon stagnation (Section 3.2). Throughout evolution,
cross-iteration fitness feedback is harvested into a cumulative importance
matrix W ∈RD governed by exponential forgetting, enabling the KER
operator to execute targeted, directional elite flips within a strictly bounded
computational budget (Section 3.3). Finally, a dynamic Two-Phase Weight
Scheduling strategy temporally decouples early coarse feature pruning from
late high-precision accuracy refinement, achieving simultaneous gains in
classification accuracy, feature compactness, and runtime efficiency (Sec-
tion 3.4). Table 1 systematically summarizes the methodological taxonomy
and structural comparison between KDS-EL and all eleven benchmark

3. The Proposed Method
To address the severe challenges of ultra-high-dimensional feature selec-
tion (D ≫103, Nsamp ≪100), this section presents the proposed KDS-EL
framework in detail. Specifically, the macro data-flow architecture and algorith-
mic flowchart of KDS-EL are outlined in Section 3.1. The prior-guided sparse
initialization and adaptive recovery strategies are detailed in Section 3.2. Sec-
tion 3.3 analyzes the search failure of conventional random perturbations, and
formulates the closed-loop mechanism of cumulative importance learning and
knowledge-guided elite refinement. The dynamic two-phase weight scheduling
strategy is presented in Section 3.4. Finally, the overall algorithmic procedure
and its computational complexity analysis are synthesized in Section 3.5.
3.1. The framework of KDS-EL
The macro data-flow architecture and problem mapping of KDS-EL for
high-dimensional feature selection are systematically illustrated in Fig. 1.
Given a high-dimensional dataset D, the search pipeline begins by extracting
filter-level mutual information to construct a continuous prior relevance
landscape. An initial swarm of continuous positions is generated via hybrid
prior seeding and deterministically mapped into sparse binary candidate
masks. Throughout optimization, these candidate subsets are transferred to the
KDS-EL optimization engine, which coordinates collaborative dual-swarm
co-evolution, dynamic two-phase fitness evaluations, cumulative feature-
importance harvesting, and targeted elite refinement in closed-loop synergy
with a wrapper classification evaluation engine. Upon meeting the convergence
criterion, the framework outputs the optimal compact biomarker subset x⋆

for τ consecutive generations.

3.3. Cumulative importance learning and elite refinement
To overcome the search amnesia and destructive perturbation bottlenecks
of conventional meta-heuristics, KDS-EL establishes a closed-loop knowledge
learning mechanism comprising online importance accumulation and targeted
directional elite refinement.
In ultra-high-dimensional feature selection (D ≫103), traditional evolu-
tionary wrappers rely on undirected stochastic bit flips with a uniform mutation
probability pmut = 1/D. Under this blind perturbation regime, meta-heuristics
inevitably suffer from two fatal structural search lesions. First, when an estab-
lished, highly informative biomarker (xe,j = 1) is blindly mutated to 0, the
delicate multi-feature synergy is abruptly dismantled, inducing severe classifi-
cation degradation. Second, when an uninformative background noise feature
(xe,j = 0) is randomly flipped to 1, the candidate subset expands unnecessar-
ily, escalating distance evaluation latency and precipitating classifier overfit-
ting. Because the probability of hitting the few decisive dimensions within a
2D combinatorial neighborhood is infinitesimal (O(1/D) ≈10−4), undirected
mutation squanders the vast majority of function evaluations on destructive tri-
als. Furthermore, conventional meta-heuristics exhibit severe search amnesia,
as historical fitness feedback from intermediate iterations is discarded immedi-
ately after particle position updates.

3.4. Dynamic two-phase weight scheduling
In high-dimensional feature selection, wrapper optimization must balance
two fundamentally conflicting objectives: minimizing classification error and
minimizing the selected feature subset cardinality. In the early evolutionary
phase, the search space is dominated by an overwhelming number of redundant
and noisy dimensions; thus, the primary optimization imperative is aggressive
dimensionality pruning. In contrast, during the late evolutionary phase when
search trajectories have converged to a compact subspace, the priority shifts
to fine-tuning synergistic biomarker interactions to maximize classification ac-
curacy. Conventional static weighting schemes with fixed trade-off coefficients
fail to reconcile these temporally distinct demands, frequently inducing either
inadequate early feature elimination or premature penalization of critical pre-
dictive features in later stages.
To resolve this temporal conflict, KDS-EL introduces the dynamic TPS
strategy, which decouples the evolutionary horizon into two distinct functional
phases. The total generation budget T is divided at a split point parameterized
by the phase transition ratio κ ∈(0, 1). The dynamic objective weight vector
w(t) = [wa(t), wf (t)] ∈[0, 1]2, satisfying wa(t) + w f (t) = 1, is formulated as:

4.2.1. Comparison of classification accuracy
The KDS-EL framework was tested with 13 comparison algorithms on 20
benchmark datasets with high-dimensional feature selection. Table 4 presents
the results of the experiments for average classification accuracy and standard
deviation, with the optimal values in bold.
First, comparing the KDS-EL algorithm to the 13 comparison algorithms
overall, KDS-EL achieved the highest average classification accuracy of
0.9574 across the benchmark suite, securing the highest score on 15 of
the 20 high-dimensional datasets. Among them, on ultra-high-dimensional
microarrays with severe sample sparsity, KDS-EL delivers substantial
performance advantages, reaching classification accuracies of 0.9522 on
the 12,625-dimensional ALL4, 0.9500 on Colon, and 0.8598 on the 54,675-
dimensional T1D, consistently establishing distinct margins over competing
single- and multi-objective algorithms. Secondly, compared to established
single-objective evolutionary algorithms such as FWPSO, PSO-CSM, SaWDE,
IGPSO, and BPSO, KDS-EL improves overall mean accuracy from the
0.8698–0.9177 range to 0.9574, indicating that closed-loop knowledge transfer
and Two-Phase Weight Scheduling effectively overcome the blind stochastic
trials of standard swarm operators. Compared with multi-objective methods
including W-MOPSO-FS, fMOPSO-FS, NSGA-II-FS, MOEA-D-FS, and
MOPSO-FS, KDS-EL maintains a consistent advantage of over 0.04 to 0.05
in mean classification score. Furthermore, compared with the original MEL
algorithm, KDS-EL achieves an accuracy improvement of 0.0706, which
indicates that mutual information initialization and knowledge-guided elite
refinement effectively enhance the algorithm’s biomarker mining capability
and local exploitation. Overall, the KDS-EL algorithm searches for a superior
subset of features and obtains higher classification accuracy.
A closer examination of the individual datasets and algorithmic dispersion
provides further insights into the search behavior and robustness of KDS-EL.
On the few moderate-dimensional datasets where KDS-EL does not record the
nominal highest accuracy, such as BASEHOCK, COIL20, and ISOLET, com-
petitors such as NSGA-II-FS achieve marginally higher accuracy by 0.003 to
0.020 only by retaining massive subsets of 185 to 2,073 features, whereas KDS-
EL attains competitive accuracy with only 60 to 134 features, achieving an
order-of-magnitude reduction in feature redundancy. On near-perfect saturated
datasets such as ALL1 and SRBCT, KDS-EL attains 0.9998 and 0.9986 accuracy
with minimal signatures of 39 and 22 features. Furthermore, KDS-EL demon-
strates remarkably low run-to-run dispersion, exhibiting a standard deviation of
only 0.0089 on Gastric1, compared with 0.0215 for FWPSO and 0.0381 for
MEL. This confirms that hybrid MI initialization and knowledge-guided elite
refinement not only deliver higher accuracy in finding optimal biomarker sub-
sets, but also substantially enhance the stability and robustness of the algorithm.
4.2.2. Comparison of the number of selected features
The KDS-EL framework was evaluated against 13 comparison algorithms
across 20 high-dimensional benchmark datasets. Table 5 presents the experi-

First, comparing the KDS-EL algorithm to the 13 comparison algorithms
overall, KDS-EL achieves the most compact feature subsets across the entire
benchmark suite, selecting an overall mean of only 87.8 features and com-
pressing the search space down to approximately 2%–3% of its original dimen-
sion. This extreme dimensionality reduction capability is especially pronounced
on challenging biomedical microarrays such as ALL1, Colon, Gastric1, and
Prostate, where KDS-EL isolates minimal biomarker signatures of fewer than
40 genes, notably 14 on Colon and 26 on Prostate, while delivering near-perfect
classification accuracies between 0.9500 and 0.9998. Secondly, compared to
specialized single-objective evolutionary algorithms, even the most compact
competitor, FWPSO, retains an average of 1,068.9 features—over 12 times
the subset size of KDS-EL—demonstrating that Two-Phase Weight Schedul-
ing successfully enforces aggressive feature elimination during early iterations.
Against the original MEL method with 2,722.2 features, KDS-EL achieves a
remarkable 31-fold reduction in feature count, confirming that mutual infor-
mation initialization and knowledge-guided elite refinement effectively steer
particles into highly sparse, discriminative subspaces rather than dense noisy
regions. Furthermore, while multi-objective paradigms like NSGA-II-FS and
fMOPSO-FS attain competitive accuracy on certain benchmarks, their opera-
tional solutions extracted via Eq. (25) retain extensive subsets ranging from
2,960.0 to 5,263.3 features. This behavior arises because unguided Pareto dom-
inance in ultra-high dimensions exerts weak selection pressure toward extreme
feature sparsity; without domain guidance, non-dominated candidate masks
containing thousands of noisy dimensions easily retain parity in training-fold
accuracy, clustering the minimum-error region of the Pareto front within overly
dense subspaces. Overall, KDS-EL discovers substantially more compact fea-
ture subsets while simultaneously securing superior classification accuracy.
From the perspective of algorithmic components, the step-wise perfor-
mance progression reveals an aggregate, macro-level compression in feature
redundancy across the benchmark suite. Averaged over all 20 benchmark
datasets, the feature count contracts from 2,722.2 in the precursor MEL
algorithm to 290.6 with mutual information seeding, further shrinks to 121.7
through knowledge-guided elite refinement, and ultimately settles at an overall
compact signature of 87.8 features under the dynamic TPS strategy. This
hierarchical reduction demonstrates that each proposed mechanism eliminates
non-informative dimensions in a complementary manner, establishing clear
overall feature-compression superiority over existing evolutionary wrappers.
Crucially, while feature reduction progresses consistently in terms of
the multi-dataset average, subset cardinality on individual problem instances
reflects a deliberate, domain-specific trade-off governed by the two-phase
scheduling dynamics. On several challenging biomedical microarrays, such as
Adenoma, Lymphoma, and SRBCT, the complete KDS-EL framework retains
moderately more features than the static-weight variant without TPS. This
behavior directly manifests the underlying design rationale of the two-phase
strategy: upon transitioning to Phase 2, the feature cardinality penalty
w f,2 = 0.05 is relaxed while the classification accuracy weight wa,2 = 0.95 is
prioritized. Rather than enforcing an inflexible sparsity constraint that risks
pruning decisive biomarker interactions, this controlled relaxation allows the
search engine to strategically retrieve a small subset of weakly individual but
strongly synergistic features that resolve ambiguous classification boundaries.
Consequently, this targeted trade-off converts marginal feature retention
into a statistically confirmed accuracy advantage across the benchmark
suite (p = 7.63 × 10−5 under the Holm–Bonferroni procedure), confirming
that KDS-EL balances extreme feature sparsity with optimal predictive
discrimination.

The sensitivity trajectories across the five hyperparameters yield critical
insights into the search dynamics and stability of KDS-EL. First, regarding the
knowledge weight decay factor γ, setting γ = 0.99 yields optimal performance
with an average accuracy of 0.944 and 107 selected features. An overly low
decay factor such as γ = 0.90 causes premature forgetting of valuable fea-
ture associations discovered in earlier iterations, degrading accuracy by 1.2%,
whereas disabling decay entirely at γ = 1.00 accumulates obsolete historical
feedback and inflates the feature count to 151 without accuracy gain. Second,
regarding the phase split ratio κ, setting κ < 0.50 allocates insufficient iterations
to Phase 1, resulting in inadequate feature pruning with 146 to 189 retained fea-
tures, while an overly prolonged Phase 1 with κ = 0.90 compresses the Phase
2 refinement window, leading to a 0.7% accuracy decline. The broad range be-
tween 0.60 and 0.80 provides a stable operating regime with accuracy variations
remaining strictly below 0.5%, confirming κ = 0.70 as an ideal balance. Third,
regarding the elite perturbation fraction ϕ, setting ϕ = 0.01 maintains an opti-
mal balance between localized exploitation and structural exploration across
heterogeneous dimensions (spanning from 5 flipped features on madelon to
546 on T1D). Because the greedy acceptance criterion (21) rigorously discards
non-improving mutations, ϕ = 0.01 provides sufficient exploratory momentum
without provoking excessive trial rejections. Conversely, increasing ϕ ≥0.05
introduces excessive perturbation that overwhelms the greedy acceptance fil-
ter, resulting in near-zero mutant admission rates, reducing classification accu-
racy by 1.3%, and inflating the selected feature count to 203. Fourth, regarding
the mutual information initialization ratio ρ, disabling MI seeding entirely at
ρ = 0.0 imposes a 1.2% accuracy penalty and increases execution time by 21%
to 62.3 seconds, while setting ρ = 1.0 homogenizes the initial population and
induces premature convergence. The default balanced setting ρ = 0.50 effec-
tively reconciles prior domain knowledge guidance with stochastic diversity.
Finally, regarding the elite pool size ke, refining only a single elite with ke = 1
restricts knowledge propagation across subpopulations, while setting ke = 10
dilutes the quality signal of matrix W, establishing ke = 3 as the optimal de-
fault.
Across all five hyperparameters, the performance trajectories in Fig. 10
demonstrate wide, forgiving basins of attraction rather than sharp, brittle
peaks. The maximum accuracy variation across the identified stable operating
bands remains strictly within 1.5%, and KDS-EL consistently outperforms the
comparison algorithms across broad parameter ranges. This demonstrates that
KDS-EL exhibits exceptional hyperparameter robustness and plug-and-play
adaptability, delivering reliable optimization performance across diverse
high-dimensional applications without requiring tedious, problem-specific
hyperparameter tuning.
4.2.8. Discussion
A critical empirical phenomenon observed in high-dimensional biomed-
ical feature selection is the accuracy ceiling effect. Among the 20 benchmark
datasets, eight gene-expression microarrays, including ALL1, ALL_AML,
Adenoma, DLBCL, Gastric2, Leukaemia, Lymphoma, and SRBCT, exhibit
classification accuracy exceeding 0.998 across top-performing methods. Under
such near-perfect classification conditions, the few available test instances
are completely separated, and accuracy differences among leading algorithms
become narrower than 0.002, corresponding to stochastic variations in at
most one cross-validation sample. Consequently, classification accuracy alone
ceases to provide discriminative utility, and the number of selected features
becomes the decisive criterion for evaluating algorithmic efficacy.
Under this saturated regime, KDS-EL demonstrates substantial superiority
in feature subset compactness, identifying ultra-sparse biomarker signatures of
only 39 to 83 features across all eight ceiling benchmarks. Specifically, KDS-
EL extracts minimal subsets of 39 features on ALL1, 45 on Lymphoma, and 64
on DLBCL. In stark contrast, single-objective FWPSO requires 278 to 1,362
features to achieve comparable accuracy, multi-objective methods retain exten-
sive subsets ranging from 700 to 10,701 features, where MOPSO-FS selects
5,920 features on ALL1 and 10,701 features on Gastric2, and the original MEL
algorithm retains 2,483 to 4,248 features. This demonstrates that conventional
evolutionary algorithms suffer from severe feature bloat in ceiling scenarios,
whereas KDS-EL effectively isolates the true minimal discriminative subset.
This dimensional contrast highlights the profound practical and clinical
relevance of KDS-EL. In translational bioinformatics and cancer diagnostics,
an ultra-compact signature of 40 to 80 biomarkers can be readily validated
through targeted RT-qPCR assays, integrated into biological pathway enrich-
ment analyses, and engineered into cost-effective diagnostic gene panels. In
contrast, candidate signatures containing hundreds or thousands of uncurated
genes are cost-prohibitive to validate clinically and introduce high risks of false
discoveries driven by spurious co-expression artifacts. By reconciling top-tier
classification accuracy, extreme biomarker sparsity, and computational scala-
bility, KDS-EL establishes a rigorous and deployable performance frontier for
high-dimensional biomedical data analytics.

Furthermore, translating computational feature selection into clinical
insight requires that the isolated dimensions reflect genuine pathophysi-
ological mechanisms rather than statistical noise. Granular inspection of
the minimal signatures identified by KDS-EL reveals strong concordance
with established cancer biology. In the Colon adenocarcinoma microarray,
top-ranked selected features map directly to well-characterized oncological
biomarkers, as detailed in Supplementary Table S10. Specifically, KDS-EL
identifies COL11A1 (probe Hsa.36689), a recognized driver of desmoplastic
stroma and tumor invasion; GUCA2A (Guanylin, probe Hsa.831), a mucosal

molecular drivers of malignancy, bridging evolutionary machine learning with
clinical translational oncology.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, the KDS-EL framework is proposed to solve the
high-dimensional feature selection problem. In KDS-EL, mutual-information-
guided initialization and adaptive re-initialization are first constructed to
establish informative search basins and restore swarm diversity during search.
In addition, to overcome unguided stochastic mutation trials, we design a
knowledge-guided elite refinement strategy to accumulate cross-iteration
feature-importance feedback, enhance candidate biomarker subset quality,
and sustain continuous optimization momentum. Furthermore, a two-phase
weight scheduling strategy temporally decouples coarse feature pruning from
fine classification refinement. Based on these collaborative mechanisms, the
experimental results of KDS-EL across 20 real-world high-dimensional bench-
mark datasets demonstrate that, compared with 13 state-of-the-art algorithms,
KDS-EL removes redundant and irrelevant features more effectively, achieves
superior classification accuracy, and substantially improves computational
efficiency.
The knowledge-guided elite refinement strategy and stage-aware weight
scheduling mechanisms in KDS-EL proposed in this paper can be used not only
to improve multi-task particle swarm optimization but also to enhance other
swarm intelligence and evolutionary algorithms. The newly proposed strate-
gies enhance the optimization capabilities of metaheuristics, aiding in provid-
ing better search methodologies for the fields of machine learning and data sci-
ence. For example, the improved evolutionary frameworks can be employed for
optimizing neural network hyperparameters, neural architecture search, multi-
omics biomarker discovery, and high-dimensional biomedical signal process-
ing, among other tasks, thereby offering novel solutions for these domains.
Future work could improve the global optimization process of evolution-
ary learning by combining relevant algorithmic ideas from machine learning
and deep representation learning, such as transfer learning and self-supervised
feature interaction modeling, to accelerate convergence and enhance scalability
on ultra-large-scale datasets. In addition, the scope of application of the algo-
rithm can also be expanded through domain-specific research. For example, the
scope of application can be explored for complex clinical challenges such as
single-cell RNA sequencing, multi-label disease diagnosis, and cancer survival
prediction, where domain-specific prior knowledge integration strategies can
be designed to improve biomarker discovery performance and clinical inter-
pretability in the target domain.
