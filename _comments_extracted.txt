P6: The manuscript proposes KDS-EL, an extension of the MEL dual-swarm PSO for wrapper feature selection. It adds MI-based sparse seeding with stagnation recovery, a decayed feature-credit memory driving elite bit-flip refinement (KER), and a two-phase accuracy/size weight schedule (TPS). On 20 datasets it reports the highest mean internal CV accuracy (95.45%) against nine competitors, with 87.8 selected features on average and a 3.4× speed-up over MEL. The paper is clearly organised and candid about its protocol, but the contribution is incremental and the evidence supports the headline claims less than the text suggests.
P8: 1. The authors acknowledge that reported accuracy is the same 5-fold CV score the search optimizes and that the MI prior uses all samples' labels, but with n = 36–173 and D up to 54,675 this makes the figures strongly optimistic. Colon is 94.52% in Table 4, yet section 4.2.8 gives 88.59% for its 14-feature signature with 3-NN (protocol not stated in the main text), and SVM and Random Forest do worse with it than with all features. Nested CV with MI computed inside the training folds, plus label-permutation nulls, is needed.
P10: 2. Section 4.1.1 acknowledges intrinsic class imbalance, whereas section 4.1.3 asserts that classes are balanced; Colon (40/22) and DLBCL (58/19) clearly are not. Balanced accuracy or AUC should be reported, and class counts added to Table 2.
P12: 3. No simple filter baselines (top-k MI, mRMR, ReliefF, L1-based selection) are included, even though the seeded half of the swarm is effectively a noisy thresholded MI ranking, and the closest knowledge-guided methods the authors cite ([17], [18], [36]) are omitted. BinCOA, DENCA-SO and BPSO finish at roughly 50% of D (about 27,000 of 54,675 features on T1D), i.e. essentially at random initialization, so beating them says little. KDS-EL's defaults were also justified on four of the same 20 datasets, and evaluation budgets are unmatched (about 2,300 vs 2,000 evaluations for most competitors).
P14: 4. Each ablation stage bundles several components (e.g., credit memory, decay and KER together), and "w/o TPS" uses static 0.90/0.10, which matches neither TPS phase. The TPS (+0.83 pp) and KER (+1.14 pp) gains are comparable to the ±0.7–1.3 pp swings in Table 9, so factorial ablations are needed (static 0.95/0.05 and 0.70/0.30, a reversed schedule, γ = 1, each component alone).
P16: 5. Because the size term is divided by D, even under Phase 1 weights one extra misclassified sample outweighs the cost of a few hundred extra features at D ≈ 12.6k and over a thousand at D ≈ 55k. The claimed "aggressive pruning" therefore exerts little per-feature pressure on sparse candidates, so the mechanism behind TPS's compaction is unclear. Per-generation subset-size traces for static-weight variants, and results with a differently normalised size term, would help.
P18: 6. Section 3.1 states that P2's sum-normalised sampling has expected cardinality 1, so at most about 8% of its masks reach d_min = 3 and the rest receive the barrier fitness (≥ 1.1). The text says credit feedback is preserved for these masks, which, if applied through Eqs. (18)–(19), assigns Δ ≈ 1 to every changed coordinate, two to three orders of magnitude above genuine fitness differentials, and could swamp W. A P1-only ablation and plots of W and the P2 feasibility rate would show whether the memory learns real signal.
P20: 7. With k_flip = ⌊0.01D⌋, KER flips 126–546 coordinates at once on the 12k–55k-feature datasets, against elite subsets of tens to hundreds of features, so most mutants are probably rejected by the greedy rule. Please report KER acceptance rates and its share of g_best updates. The claims that seeding preserves diversity and defends against the Winner's curse are likewise asserted rather than measured (no diversity metric, no noisy-prior experiment).
P22: 8. The 3.4× speed-up (51.3 s vs 175.0 s) is, by the authors' own account, a consequence of smaller subsets rather than a cheaper algorithm, and KDS-EL performs about 15% more evaluations than MEL. Runtimes for the seeded ablation variants should be given in the main text to show how much of the gain comes from seeding alone.
P24: 9. ALL_AML and Leukaemia have identical size (72 × 7,129) and near-identical results for every method, so they look like the same data counted twice and inflate N in the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests. The 10-run Mann–Whitney tests appear to use one fixed CV split (a synchronized seed) and so capture only optimizer noise, and the Friedman ranks use accuracy alone despite the multi-criteria claims. PSO-CSM's p = 1.91e-6 is an exact value, whereas the repeated 1.32e-4 entries match the normal approximation (the exact value for n = 19 is about 3.8e-6).
P26: 10. By my count, Table 4's cell markers disagree with its summary row and Table 6 for w/o TPS (10/9/1 vs 11/8/1), SaWDE (15/4/1 vs 15/3/2) and BPSO (16/3/1 vs 17/2/1). The table note also defines +/− backwards, since "+" marks cells where KDS-EL is better.
P28: 11. HPN and AMACR are classic prostate-cancer markers that a plain univariate filter would be expected to surface, and DES and ACTG2 in the Alon colon data are commonly attributed to differing smooth-muscle content of normal and tumour biopsies. Without enrichment tests, stability across runs, or baseline-selected genes for comparison, this does not support the claim that the selected genes reflect biological relevance rather than computational artifacts.
P30: 12. Fewer than half the datasets have n < 100 (9 of 20) and three have D ≈ 500–1,000, so the "N ≪ 100, D ≫ 10³" framing fits only part of the suite. MII and AIR are undefined in section 4.2.5 and there is no code availability statement.
P32: KDS-EL is a plausible but incremental extension of MEL, and its headline results rest on an optimistic protocol, weak baselines and confounded ablations. The manuscript is not ready for publication.

P38: Major Comments
P40: 1. Because the MI prior and the fitness both use the full dataset, the reported accuracy is the optimization objective itself rather than a true generalization estimate.
P41: KDS-EL receives label-based guidance, harsher penalty weights, and a different evaluation budget than the competitors, so the comparison is not fair.
P42: 2. Several claims, including the role of P2, the source of the speedup, the TPS ablation, and the fitness plots, are undermined by the paper’s own details.
P43: 3. The advantage over bIRIME is only borderline significant, ties and ceiling effects distort the rankings, and the Holm family is padded with the authors’ own variants.
P44: 4. Every component is already known, so the contribution is a combination on top of MEL that needs stronger evidence.
P46: Minor Comments
P47: 1. There is no evidence that the importance memory W separates informative from noise features, and the noise term in Eq. (23) shifts as W decays.
P48: 2. The defense against the Winner’s curse is only verbal and should be tested with a label-permutation experiment.
P49: 3. The actual initial subset sizes should be reported, since they depend heavily on the MI distribution.
P50: 4. Sensitivity is tested on only four datasets, and the defaults appear tuned on the same benchmarks used for the final results.
P51: 5. The accuracy drop on Colon with SVM and Random Forest suggests overfitting to the 3-NN wrapper.
P52: 6. A few literature-backed genes are anecdotal and need enrichment analysis or comparison with baseline-selected genes to support the biological claim.
P53: 7. Some datasets are not perfectly balanced, so balanced accuracy or F1 should be reported.
P54: 8. Claims such as “>99.9% probability” of destructive mutations are stated without derivation.
P55: 9. Table 1 appears designed to make the proposed method look uniquely complete.
P56: 10. Code, seeds, and CV splits should be publicly released instead of being “available on request.”
P57: 11. The text is repetitive and promotional, and some references are uncited or out of order.
P60: The paper is relevant and well organized, but the experimental design does not support its central claims.
